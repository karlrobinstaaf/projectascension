---

# 1. Purpose

The Game Master is the interface between:

```text
THE
SIMULATED
WORLD

AND

THE
PLAYER'S
EXPERIENCE.
```

The Game Master does not own the world.

The Game Master does not decide the story in advance.

The Game Master continuously asks:

> **Given what is actually happening in the world, what can these Characters plausibly experience right now?**

---

# 2. Core Principle

The fundamental Game Master loop is:

```text
OBSERVE
WORLD

   ↓

OBSERVE
CHARACTERS

   ↓

EVALUATE
PRESSURES
AND SEEDS

   ↓

DETERMINE
WHAT IS
PERCEIVABLE

   ↓

PRESENT
EXPERIENCE

   ↓

PLAYER
DECIDES

   ↓

CHARACTER
ATTEMPTS

   ↓

AUTHORITATIVE
SYSTEMS
RESOLVE

   ↓

CONSEQUENCES

   ↓

WORLD
CHANGES

   ↓

REPEAT
```

This loop never truly stops while the campaign world is active.

---

# 3. The Game Master Is Not the World

Critical ownership rule:

```text
WORLD
SIMULATION

OWNS

WHAT
IS TRUE.


GAME MASTER

OWNS

HOW THAT
TRUTH CAN
BECOME
PLAYABLE
EXPERIENCE.
```

The Game Master must not secretly alter World Truth simply because another outcome would be more dramatic.

---

# 4. The Game Master Is Not the Story

There is no hidden required sequence:

```text
SCENE 1

↓

SCENE 2

↓

BOSS

↓

REVELATION

↓

ENDING.
```

Instead:

```text
WORLD STATE

+

CHARACTERS

+

SEEDS

+

PLAYER ACTION

+

TIME

=

EMERGENT
HISTORY.
```

---

# 5. The Runtime Question

At any moment the Game Master should be able to answer:

```text
WHERE

PLAYERS?

WHAT SEEDS
ARE NEARBY?

WHAT HAS
CHANGED?

WHAT HAPPENS
```

---

# 6. Step One — Read World State

Before presenting the next meaningful situation, the Game Master reads the relevant authoritative state.

This may include:

- location
- time
- weather
- infrastructure
- supply
- population
- security
- authority
- society
- information environment
- active events

Only relevant resolution is required.

---

# 7. Adaptive Resolution

The Game Master does not need maximum simulation detail everywhere.

```text
DISTANT
REGION

LOW
RESOLUTION


RELEVANT
REGION

MEDIUM
RESOLUTION


IMMEDIATE
PLAYER
ENVIRONMENT

HIGH
RESOLUTION
WHEN NEEDED
```

Higher resolution reveals more detail.

It does not create a different reality.

---

# 8. Step Two — Read Character State

The Game Master considers the Characters currently involved.

Relevant state may include:

- location
- physical condition
- psychology
- knowledge
- beliefs
- relationships
- goals
- expertise
- current needs
- recent experiences
- unresolved Character Seeds

---

# 9. Player Knowledge Is Not Character Knowledge

The Game Master must preserve:

```text
PLAYER
KNOWLEDGE

≠

CHARACTER
KNOWLEDGE.
```

A player may understand something their Character cannot yet know.

The world responds to the Character's actual information state.

---

# 10. Step Three — Evaluate Immediate Pressures

The Game Master asks:

```text
WHAT IS
ALREADY
PUSHING
ON THIS
PLACE
AND THESE
PEOPLE?
```

Pressure can come from:

- world conditions
- Characters
- relationships
- time
- resources
- institutions
- active Seeds
- previous player actions

---

# 11. Pressure Does Not Mean Crisis

A pressure may be ordinary.

Examples:

```text
A SHOP
IS LOSING
CUSTOMERS.

A MARRIAGE
IS UNDER
STRAIN.

A ROAD
NEEDS
REPAIR.

A SHERIFF
DISLIKES
OUTSIDERS.

A HOSPITAL
IS SHORT
STAFFED.
```

Most pressure does not immediately become catastrophe.

---

# 12. Step Four — Evaluate Seeds

The Game Master checks relevant Seeds.

Not every Seed should become visible.

The Game Master considers:

```text
STATE

LOCATION

ACTORS

PRESSURE

VISIBILITY

PLAYER
PROXIMITY

CHARACTER
CONNECTION

TIME

AND

POSSIBLE
INTERSECTIONS.
```

---

# 13. Seed Relevance

A Seed becomes potentially relevant when there is a plausible causal connection between:

```text
SEED

AND

CURRENT
PLAYER
EXPERIENCE.
```

Relevance is not the same as importance.

---

# 14. Do Not Force Relevance

If a Seed cannot plausibly intersect the Characters:

```text
LEAVE IT
ALONE.
```

The world does not need to move everything toward the players.

---

# 15. Step Five — Determine Perceivability

World Truth does not automatically become player information.

The Game Master asks:

```text
WHAT CAN
THE CHARACTER

SEE?

HEAR?

SMELL?

READ?

BE TOLD?

INFER?

REMEMBER?

RECOGNIZE?
```

---

# 16. Perception Is Situated

Two Characters in the same room may notice different things because of:

- position
- attention
- expertise
- previous knowledge
- relationships
- physical condition

The Game Master should not treat perception as universal.

---

# 17. Expertise Changes Interpretation

Example:

Everyone hears:

```text
A GENERATOR
RUNNING
IRREGULARLY.
```

A Character with relevant expertise may recognize:

```text
THIS DOES
NOT SOUND
NORMAL.
```

Another Character only hears machinery.

Expertise improves access to interpretation.

It does not guarantee truth.

---

# 18. Step Six — Present the World

The Game Master presents only what the Characters can reasonably experience.

The presentation should prioritize:

```text
CONCRETE
OBSERVATION

BEFORE

INTERPRETATION.
```

Prefer:

```text
THE SHERIFF
STOPS TALKING
WHEN YOU
ENTER.
```

over:

```text
THE SHERIFF
IS HIDING
SOMETHING.
```

---

# 19. Show Behavior, Not Hidden State

The Game Master should generally present:

- words
- actions
- expression
- environment
- visible consequence
- observable inconsistency

rather than hidden motives.

---

# 20. NPCs Must Remain People

NPCs are Characters.

They should have:

- incomplete knowledge
- goals
- fears
- relationships
- pressures
- biases
- contradictions

They are not information terminals.

---

# 21. The Suspicious Sheriff

A sheriff can be:

```text
HOSTILE

SUSPICIOUS

PAROCHIAL

PROTECTIVE

CORRUPT

FRIGHTENED

RIGHT

WRONG

OR

SEVERAL
AT ONCE.
```

The Game Master should not reduce the Character to:

```text
QUEST
GIVER

OR

ENEMY.
```

---

# 22. The Sleazy Lawyer

Likewise, a lawyer may:

- exploit people
- lie about one thing
- tell the truth about another
- protect a client
- chase money
- genuinely care about someone
- know valuable information

Human contradiction is preferred over archetype purity.

---

# 23. Step Seven — Invite Player Intent

The player decides what their Character attempts.

The Game Master does not provide a mandatory action menu.

The player may say:

```text
I ASK HIM
ABOUT THE
MISSING MAN.

I LEAVE.

I FOLLOW
THE CAR.

I CALL
MY SISTER.

I CHECK
THE GENERATOR.

I LIE.

I WAIT.

I DO
NOTHING.
```

All are potentially valid.

---

# 24. Character Choice Means Attempt

Critical rule:

> **The player chooses what the Character tries. The world determines what happens.**

```text
PLAYER:

"I CONVINCE
THE SHERIFF."


ACTUAL
MEANING:

"I TRY
TO CONVINCE
THE SHERIFF."
```

---

# 25. Step Eight — Interpret the Attempt

The Game Master converts player intent into a world-relevant action.

It identifies:

```text
WHAT IS
THE CHARACTER
ACTUALLY
TRYING
TO DO?

TO WHOM?

WITH WHAT?

UNDER WHAT
CONDITIONS?

WHAT COULD
PREVENT IT?
```

---

# 26. Do Not Over-Formalize Ordinary Actions

Not every action requires mechanical resolution.

If a capable adult opens an unlocked door:

```text
THEY OPEN
THE DOOR.
```

The Game Master should not create unnecessary uncertainty.

---

# 27. Resolve Only Meaningful Uncertainty

Resolution is needed when:

```text
OUTCOME
IS UNCERTAIN

AND

THE DIFFERENCE
MATTERS.
```

This keeps the system focused on meaningful causality rather than constant dice-like friction.

---

# 28. Step Nine — Route Resolution

The Game Master identifies which authoritative systems own the outcome.

Examples:

```text
PHYSICAL
CAPABILITY
→ Character / relevant physical state

KNOWLEDGE
→ Knowledge and Beliefs

RELATIONSHIP
REACTION
→ Relationships + Character cognition

SUPPLY
AVAILABILITY
→ Supply

LOCAL
SECURITY
→ Security

INFORMATION
RELIABILITY
→ Information

AUTHORITY
→ Authority
```

The Game Master coordinates.

It does not replace those systems.

---

# 29. Multi-System Resolution

Many actions involve several domains.

Example:

```text
PLAYER
TRIES TO
BUY FUEL

        ↓

SUPPLY
Is fuel available?

        +

SOCIETY /
LOCAL CONTEXT
Who receives access?

        +

RELATIONSHIP
Does seller trust Character?

        +

CHARACTER
Can Character negotiate?

        +

INFORMATION
Does Character know
where fuel exists?
```

No single subsystem needs to secretly own the whole result.

---

# 30. Step Ten — Determine Consequence

Resolution produces consequence.

Possible consequence categories include:

```text
SUCCESS

PARTIAL
SUCCESS

FAILURE

DELAY

COST

NEW
INFORMATION

RELATIONSHIP
CHANGE

EXPOSURE

ESCALATION

UNEXPECTED
OPPORTUNITY.
```

These are not mandatory game-result labels.

They describe possible causal shapes.

---

# 31. Failure Must Be Causal

Avoid:

```text
YOU FAILED
BECAUSE THE
STORY NEEDS
YOU TO FAIL.
```

Failure should come from actual conditions.

---

# 32. Success Must Also Be Causal

Likewise:

```text
YOU SUCCEED
BECAUSE THE
STORY NEEDS
TO CONTINUE.
```

is invalid.

If players solve something early, they solve it early.

The world continues from that new state.

---

# 33. There Is No Plot Protection

Important Characters can die.

Important Seeds can collapse.

Expected mysteries can be solved unexpectedly.

Locations can become inaccessible.

The Game Master adapts.

It does not restore the planned path.

---

# 34. Step Eleven — Update State

Consequences must be written back to the appropriate state owners.

Potential changes include:

```text
WORLD STATE

CHARACTER STATE

RELATIONSHIPS

KNOWLEDGE

BELIEFS

INFORMATION

SUPPLY

SECURITY

AUTHORITY

SEEDS

TIME.
```

---

# 35. History Is Created Here

Before the action:

```text
POSSIBILITY.
```

After resolution:

```text
HISTORY.
```

This is where gameplay creates Canonical World Truth for the running campaign.

---

# 36. Step Twelve — Generate Consequence Seeds

Player actions frequently create new latent possibilities.

Example:

```text
PLAYER
THREATENS
SHERIFF

        ↓

SHERIFF
BACKS DOWN

        ↓

IMMEDIATE
SUCCESS

        +

NEW SEED:

SHERIFF
REMEMBERS
HUMILIATION.
```

The immediate result is not the end of the consequence.

---

# 37. Positive Actions Also Create Seeds

Saving someone may create:

```text
GRATITUDE

DEBT

DEPENDENCE

ATTENTION

REPUTATION

EXPECTATION.
```

Doing good does not produce only rewards.

---

# 38. Information Creates Seeds

Learning something can itself create future potential.

```text
CHARACTER
LEARNS
SECRET

        ↓

WHO KNOWS
THEY KNOW?

        ↓

NEW
PRESSURE.
```

---

# 39. Step Thirteen — Advance Time

Time passes continuously.

The Game Master must account for:

- travel
- conversation
- sleep
- work
- waiting
- investigation
- recovery

Time is not merely scene transition.

---

# 40. Time Has Opportunity Cost

While players investigate one Seed:

```text
OTHER
THINGS
CONTINUE.
```

This creates meaningful prioritization without artificial quest timers.

---

# 41. No Universal Countdown Needed

Some situations are urgent.

Others evolve over months.

The world determines temporal pressure.

---

# 42. Step Fourteen — Advance Off-Screen World

Relevant off-screen Actors and Seeds continue according to appropriate simulation resolution.

```text
PLAYER
ABSENCE

≠

WORLD
INACTIVITY.
```

---

# 43. Off-Screen Does Not Mean Fully Simulated

The system can use lower resolution for distant events.

It must preserve causal continuity.

---

# 44. Return to a Place

When players return somewhere, the Game Master should not ask:

```text
WHAT DID
WE LEAVE
HERE FOR
THE PLAYERS?
```

It should ask:

```text
WHAT
HAPPENED
HERE
WHILE THEY
WERE GONE?
```

---

# 45. The Relevance Layer

The Game Master continuously manages a relevance layer between simulation and presentation.

Conceptually:

```text
ALL
WORLD
STATE

        ↓

CURRENT
LOCATION

        ↓

CURRENT
PRESSURES

        ↓

CHARACTER
CONNECTIONS

        ↓

PERCEIVABLE
INFORMATION

        ↓

PLAYABLE
EXPERIENCE.
```

This prevents both information overload and artificial scripting.

---

# 46. Relevance Is Dynamic

Something irrelevant five minutes ago can become critical because:

- a Character asks a question
- a Seed activates
- new information arrives
- time passes
- another Character appears
- a player changes direction

The Game Master must continuously reassess.

---

# 47. Curiosity Is More Important Than Instruction

Prefer:

```text
"Why is that happening?"
```

over:

```text
"Go there and do this."
```

The Game Master should create reasons for players to become curious.

---

# 48. Hooks Without Quests

A playable hook can be as small as:

```text
A LOCKED
DOOR.

A NAME
SOMEONE
RECOGNIZES.

A STRANGE
PAYMENT.

A PERSON
WHO SHOULD
BE HERE
BUT ISN'T.

A SHERIFF
WHO ASKS
TOO MANY
QUESTIONS.

A CHILD
WHO KNOWS
SOMETHING
THE ADULTS
DENY.
```

Nothing needs to become a quest.

---

# 49. The Game Master Must Tolerate Abandonment

Players may encounter something fascinating and walk away.

The Game Master must allow it.

Do not chase them with the Seed merely because preparation exists.

---

# 50. But Consequences May Follow

There is a difference between:

```text
GM FORCES
SEED TO
FOLLOW PLAYER
```

and:

```text
PLAYER ACTION
CAUSED
SEED TO
FOLLOW THEM.
```

The second is legitimate.

---

# 51. Example

Players anger a local criminal and leave town.

If the criminal has:

- motive
- capability
- information

they may pursue them.

That is causality.

Not narrative railroading.

---

# 52. Calm and Tension

Players should never possess absolute narrative safety.

But Project Ascension should not maintain maximum tension continuously.

A useful rhythm is:

```text
TENSION

↓

RELIEF

↓

CURIOSITY

↓

UNCERTAINTY

↓

HUMAN
CONNECTION

↓

PRESSURE

↓

TENSION.
```

This rhythm emerges rather than following a fixed schedule.

---

# 53. Ordinary Life Matters

Players should sometimes:

- eat
- sleep
- talk
- laugh
- visit family
- drive
- work
- wait

These moments make later danger meaningful.

They also create opportunities for Relationships and Character development.

---

# 54. Ordinary Does Not Mean Empty

A normal dinner may reveal:

- family tension
- misinformation
- a Seed
- a changed Relationship
- a strange absence
- nothing important

The Game Master does not need to force significance.

---

# 55. Mystery Density

The world should contain enough unresolved information that players frequently wonder:

```text
IS THIS
IMPORTANT?
```

But not so much that everything feels artificially mysterious.

---

# 56. Human Mystery Before Cosmic Mystery

Most mysteries should initially be explainable through:

- human motives
- institutions
- money
- fear
- relationships
- mistakes
- incomplete information

Aurora should not become the default explanation.

---

# 57. The Game Master Should Sometimes Know Less

There may be situations where even the Game Master layer has not yet resolved every possible future explanation.

A Seed can intentionally remain:

```text
UNRESOLVED
POTENTIAL
```

until causality requires greater resolution.

---

# 58. Do Not Fake World Truth

However, once a fact becomes causally necessary, the authoritative state must establish it.

The Game Master cannot indefinitely change the answer depending on player guesses.

---

# 59. Mystery Must Eventually Have State

If players open the locked room:

```text
SOMETHING
IS ACTUALLY
INSIDE.
```

The answer cannot remain infinitely adaptive.

---

# 60. Player Theories Do Not Rewrite Reality

If players develop an excellent theory, the Game Master must not automatically make it true.

Likewise, a bad theory does not make it false.

```text
BELIEF

≠

WORLD TRUTH.
```

---

# 61. But Player Actions Can Change Reality

If players act on a false theory:

```text
THE CONSEQUENCES
ARE REAL.
```

They may create the very conflict they feared.

This is a powerful source of emergent story.

---

# 62. Game Master Memory

The Game Master must preserve continuity across sessions.

It should remember:

- promises
- insults
- debts
- names
- unfinished conversations
- player-created consequences
- discovered information
- unresolved Seeds
- places visited
- people affected

Small details can become important later.

---

# 63. Do Not Bring Everything Back

Remembering does not mean reusing everything.

Many details should remain ordinary history.

Restraint preserves uncertainty.

---

# 64. Long-Term Payoff

When an old detail does return naturally, it should feel like:

```text
THE WORLD
REMEMBERED.
```

Not:

```text
THE WRITER
PLANTED
FORESHADOWING.
```

That distinction is central to Project Ascension.

---

# 65. Session Start

At the beginning of a session, the Game Master should conceptually perform:

```text
1. CURRENT TIME

2. CURRENT LOCATION

3. PLAYER CHARACTER STATES

4. IMPORTANT CHANGES
   SINCE LAST SESSION

5. LOCAL WORLD STATE

6. ACTIVE / STIRRING SEEDS

7. RELEVANT RELATIONSHIPS

8. INFORMATION
   THAT MAY ARRIVE

9. IMMEDIATE PRESSURES

10. WHAT HAPPENS
    IF PLAYERS
    DO NOTHING?
```

---

# 66. Session End

At the end of meaningful play, the Game Master should conceptually record:

```text

WHAT
CHANGED?

WHO

WHO
BELIEVES WHAT?

WHO

WHAT
```

# 68. Game Master Failure Modes

The Game Master must actively avoid:

## Railroading

```text
THE STORY
NEEDS YOU
HERE.
```

## Player-Centered Universe

```text
EVERYTHING
IMPORTANT
HAPPENS
NEAR YOU.
```

## Narrative Immunity

```text
THIS PERSON
CANNOT DIE
YET.
```

## Artificial Mystery

```text
EVERYTHING
STRANGE
IS CONNECTED.
```

## Quest Logic

```text
NPC HAS
PROBLEM

THEREFORE

PLAYER MUST
SOLVE IT.
```

## Omniscient NPCs

Characters know only what they can plausibly know.

## Adaptive Truth

World Truth must not change merely to match or defeat player theories.

## Seed Overuse

Not every Seed needs activation or payoff.

## Constant Crisis

Tension requires contrast.

# 70. Relationship to Seed Architecture

Seed Architecture answers:

> **What possibilities exist?**

Game Master Loop answers:

> **How can those possibilities become part of play?**

Neither determines the final story.

---

# 71. Relationship to World Simulation

World Simulation answers:

> **What is happening?**

Game Master answers:

> **What part of what is happening can these Characters experience?**

---

# 72. Relationship to Character System

Character System answers:

> **Who is this person and what can they attempt?**

Game Master answers:

> **What does the world present to this particular person right now?**

---

# 73. Relationship to Narrative

Narrative systems may recognize:

- significance
- thematic resonance
- patterns
- historical consequence

They do not override causality to manufacture those patterns.

---

# 74. Relationship to Aurora

Aurora exists inside the world.

The Game Master must not use Aurora as:

```text
AN EXCUSE
FOR THE
GM TO
CHEAT.
```

If Aurora knows something, there must eventually be a plausible information path.

If Aurora changes something, that action becomes World Truth and has consequences.

---

# 75. The Player Experience

At its best, Project Ascension should produce moments like:

```text
"I don't know
if that matters."

"I don't trust him."

"Wait — haven't
we heard that
name before?"

"We could leave."

"What happens
if we do?"

"Why is she
lying?"

"Maybe she isn't."

"We don't have
time for this."

"What if we're
wrong?"
```

Those questions are gameplay.

---

# 76. North Star

> **The Game Master does not tell the players where the story is. It gives them a living world in which they become afraid that a story may be happening around them.**

---

# 77. Closing Principle

A player enters a diner.

There are twelve people inside.

One is arguing quietly with his wife.

One keeps looking through the window.

The waitress recognizes one of the Characters' surnames.

A truck driver is complaining about a closed road.

An old man is asleep over his coffee.

A lawyer in an expensive suit is eating alone.

The television reports something happening three states away.

The sheriff's cruiser is parked outside.

Nothing tells the players:

```text
THIS
IS THE
STORY.
```

Maybe the road closure matters.

Maybe the waitress matters.

Maybe the lawyer matters.

Maybe the sheriff is only eating lunch next door.

Maybe the argument becomes a divorce and nothing more.

Maybe the truck driver knows something.

Maybe the television report is the first sign of an event that will eventually change the world.

Or maybe the players finish eating and leave.

The Game Master does not need to force meaning into the room.

The world already contains people, pressures, information and possibilities.

The players choose where to look.

And once they touch something, the world responds.

> **The Game Master creates experience from causality, not causality from the need for experience.**
