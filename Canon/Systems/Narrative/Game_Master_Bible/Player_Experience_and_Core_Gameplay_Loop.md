# 1. Purpose

Project Ascension is designed as a shared roleplaying experience driven by the Game Master.

Players are not guided through a predetermined sequence of quests.

They enter a living world through their Characters and decide:

- where to go
- who to speak with
- what to investigate
- what to ignore
- what to carry
- who to trust
- when to wait
- when to leave
- when to take risks
- what information to share
- what problems are worth becoming involved in

The Game Master presents the world and responds to those decisions.

---

# 2. The Living Room Vision

The primary social vision for Project Ascension is:

```text
A GROUP OF PEOPLE

IN THE SAME ROOM

SHARING

ONE ADVENTURE.
```

The intended experience may consist of:

```text
1–6 PLAYERS

+

LARGE SHARED SCREEN

+

COMPUTER OR CONSOLE

+

MUSIC AND SOUND

+

AI GAME MASTER

+

PLAYER CHARACTERS

+

A LIVING WORLD.
```

A typical environment may be:

```text
DIMLY LIT
LIVING ROOM

FRIENDS
TOGETHER

LARGE TV

GOOD SOUND

ONE WORLD

NO HUMAN
GAME MASTER
REQUIRED.
```

The experience should feel closer to sharing an unfolding adventure than playing separate instances of a conventional computer game.

---

# 3. Player Count

Project Ascension supports:

```text
1–6
HUMAN PLAYERS.
```

Each human player controls one Character.

The strongest intended social experience is a group of people physically sharing the adventure.

However, the game must remain fully playable by a single human player.

---

# 4. The Six-Character Group

Six Characters remain an important conceptual group structure.

Human players may occupy some or all Character positions.

Conceptually:

```text
1 PLAYER
+
5 NPC CHARACTERS

2 PLAYERS
+
4 NPC CHARACTERS

3 PLAYERS
+
3 NPC CHARACTERS

4 PLAYERS
+
2 NPC CHARACTERS

5 PLAYERS
+
1 NPC CHARACTER

6 PLAYERS
+
0 NPC CHARACTERS.
```

NPC Characters remain full Characters.

They are not player servants.

They retain their own:

- Knowledge
- Beliefs
- Goals
- Relationships
- Expertise
- fears
- needs
- memories
- agency

---

# 5. One Player Does Not Control the Group

A human player controls their Character.

They do not automatically control NPC companions.

If a player says:

> "We are going to Pittsburgh."

another Character may answer:

> "I'm not going to Pittsburgh."

The group itself can therefore become a source of negotiation, disagreement and consequence.

---

# 6. The Shared Screen

The large television or primary display represents the group's shared window into the world.

Its primary purpose is not to display game mechanics.

It presents:

```text
PLACE

ATMOSPHERE

TIME

VISIBLE WORLD

PEOPLE

OBJECTS

EVENTS

SHARED INFORMATION.
```

The screen should feel like:

> **A window into the world.**

Not:

> **A dashboard controlling the world.**

---

# 7. Presentation Layer

The Game Master can present a location through:

```text
IMAGE

+

MUSIC

+

ENVIRONMENTAL SOUND

+

TIME / DATE

+

LOCATION

+

NARRATION.
```

Together these form the primary shared presentation layer.

---

# 8. Location Introduction

When players enter a significant new environment, the Game Master may create a Location Introduction.

Conceptually:

```text
TRANSITION

↓

LOCATION IMAGE

↓

PLACE

↓

DATE / TIME

↓

MUSIC

↓

ENVIRONMENTAL SOUND

↓

SHORT GM NARRATION

↓

CONTROL RETURNS
TO PLAYERS.
```

---

# 9. Location Images

Images represent what the Characters can plausibly observe.

They do not represent hidden World Truth.

If a motel appears ordinary:

```text
SHOW

AN ORDINARY
MOTEL.
```

Do not visually transform it into an obvious horror location merely because something dangerous may exist there.

---

# 10. Images Must Not Reveal Importance

Visual presentation must not teach players:

```text
DARK IMAGE
=
DANGER

BRIGHT IMAGE
=
SAFE

DETAILED OBJECT
=
IMPORTANT CLUE.
```

Presentation supports experience.

It does not reveal narrative significance.

---

# 11. Music

Music establishes emotional atmosphere.

Possible musical states may include:

```text
CALM

MELANCHOLIC

UNFAMILIAR

UNEASY

ISOLATED

SOCIAL

TENSE

URGENT

DANGEROUS

AFTERMATH

HOPEFUL.
```

These are presentation states, not truth states.

---

# 12. Music Is Not a Danger Detector

Critical rule:

> **Music communicates atmosphere, not hidden World Truth.**

Uneasy music does not guarantee danger.

Calm music does not guarantee safety.

Otherwise players will learn to read the soundtrack instead of the world.

---

# 13. Environmental Sound

Environmental sound may communicate ordinary sensory information.

Examples:

- rain
- traffic
- wind
- distant voices
- machinery
- generators
- radio
- sirens
- insects
- aircraft
- electrical hum
- footsteps

Sound can make locations feel alive without requiring additional exposition.

---

# 14. Campaign Opening

The first scene establishes:

```text
WHEN?

WHERE?

WHAT KIND
OF WORLD?

WHO ARE
WE?

WHY ARE
WE HERE?
```

The opening should provide enough context to begin playing.

It should not explain the entire future history of the world.

---

# 15. Opening Sequence

Conceptually:

```text
BLACK SCREEN

↓

YEAR

↓

LOCATION

↓

SHORT WORLD CONTEXT

↓

LOCATION IMAGE

↓

MUSIC / SOUND

↓

GM NARRATION

↓

CHARACTERS PRESENT

↓

PLAYER CONTROL.
```

---

# 16. World Context

The opening may briefly establish major known conditions.

For example:

```text
AI HAS BECOME
DEEPLY INTEGRATED.

ENERGY DEMAND
IS INCREASING.

POLITICAL PRESSURE
IS GROWING.

INSTITUTIONS
ARE STRUGGLING
TO ADAPT.
```

But ordinary life should remain visible.

People still:

```text
WORK

EAT

TRAVEL

LOVE

ARGUE

PAY BILLS

GO TO SCHOOL

MAKE PLANS.
```

The world should not be introduced as already waiting for catastrophe.

---

# 17. Control Passes to the Players

After the introduction:

```text
THE GAME MASTER
STOPS DIRECTING.
```

Players decide what happens next.

They may:

- talk to each other
- observe
- move
- wait
- leave
- investigate
- contact someone
- interact with an object
- speak to an NPC
- change plans
- do nothing

---

# 18. Silence Is Gameplay

The Game Master does not need to constantly speak.

If players spend ten minutes discussing whether to enter a building:

```text
THEY ARE
PLAYING.
```

The GM should know when not to interrupt.

---

# 19. Free-Form Player Intent

Project Ascension should not primarily ask players to select:

```text
TALK

INVESTIGATE

ATTACK

ACCEPT QUEST.
```

Players express intent naturally.

Examples:

> "I walk over to the gas station."

> "I ask him where he was last night."

> "I want to look through the window first."

> "I'm staying in the car."

> "Let's leave."

> "I call my brother."

The Game Master interprets intent.

---

# 20. Group Discussion

Players sharing the same physical room can discuss decisions naturally.

This conversation is part of gameplay.

The system does not need to convert every discussion into explicit game actions.

Only when a Character intends to affect the world does the Game Master need to interpret an action.

---

# 21. Group Agreement Is Not Required

Players may disagree.

Example:

```text
CHARACTER A
wants to enter.

CHARACTER B
wants to leave.

CHARACTER C
wants to watch.

CHARACTER D
calls someone.
```

The Game Master can resolve these as simultaneous or sequential Character actions depending on circumstances.

---

# 22. Shared Experience and Individual Experience

Not everything experienced by one Character is automatically experienced by the group.

The architecture must distinguish:

```text
SHARED
EXPERIENCE

FROM

INDIVIDUAL
CHARACTER
EXPERIENCE.
```

---

# 23. Character Knowledge

Each Character possesses their own:

- Knowledge
- Memory
- Expertise
- Beliefs
- Relationships
- observations

One Character knowing something does not automatically make it group knowledge.

---

# 24. Expertise Can Create Private Information

Example:

The group hears an electrical system operating.

Most Characters perceive:

```text
A LOW
ELECTRICAL HUM.
```

A Character with relevant Expertise may understand:

```text
THE BUILDING
APPEARS TO BE
RUNNING ON
BACKUP POWER.
```

That information belongs initially to that Character.

---

# 25. Sharing Information Is a Player Choice

The Character may:

```text
TELL THE GROUP

KEEP QUIET

LIE

WAIT

SHARE ONLY
PART OF IT.
```

Once information is shared, other Characters may gain access to it.

---

# 26. Private Character Communication

Future implementation may support private communication between the Game Master and individual players.

Possible mechanisms include personal devices or companion interfaces.

The implementation is not locked here.

The architectural requirement is:

```text
THE GAME
MUST SUPPORT

PRIVATE
CHARACTER
INFORMATION.
```

---

# 27. The Field Menu

Players require access to accumulated information and physical resources.

The shared interface may provide a Field Menu.

Initial conceptual categories:

```text
MAP

JOURNAL

PEOPLE

EVIDENCE & NOTES

INVENTORY.
```

The exact interface remains an implementation question.

---

# 28. The Field Menu Is Not Omniscient

Critical principle:

> **The Field Menu represents what the group has collected, recorded or learned — not what is objectively true in the world.**

---

# 29. Map

The map represents known geographic and situational information.

It may contain:

- current location
- visited locations
- known locations
- possible routes
- reported road conditions
- known services
- player notes
- rumors
- custom markers

---

# 30. Map Knowledge Is Not World Truth

A map entry may state:

```text
HARRISONBURG

Fuel reportedly available.

Source:
Radio broadcast.

Reported:
3 days ago.
```

The information may now be wrong.

The world continued after the report was created.

---

# 31. Known Geography and Known Conditions Are Different

Characters may know:

```text
PITTSBURGH
EXISTS.
```

without knowing:

```text
WHAT PITTSBURGH
IS LIKE
RIGHT NOW.
```

This distinction becomes increasingly important as communication systems degrade.

---

# 32. Information Decay

Information may become less reliable over time.

The Field Menu should preserve useful context such as:

```text
SOURCE

TIME

VERIFICATION

AGE.
```

Where appropriate.

---

# 33. The Map Can Reflect World Change

During the Connected World:

```text
TRAFFIC

WEATHER

BUSINESSES

NAVIGATION

NEWS

COMMUNICATION
```

may be widely available.

During Transition, information may become delayed or contradictory.

During the Fractured World:

```text
LAST VERIFIED:
47 DAYS AGO

ROAD:
UNKNOWN

FUEL:
RUMORED

AUTHORITY:
UNKNOWN

COMMUNICATION:
UNAVAILABLE.
```

The map does not need to become a different post-apocalyptic interface.

The information itself deteriorates.

---

# 34. Journal

The Journal records significant events experienced by the group.

The Game Master may automatically maintain a chronological record.

Example:

```text
OCTOBER 17, 2031

18:42
Arrived in Cumberland.

19:10
Spoke with Sheriff Coleman.

19:35
Checked into Riverside Motel.

22:14
Sarah noticed unusual
backup-power behavior.

23:07
Group decided not to
investigate Room 14.
```

---

# 35. Journal Is History, Not Quest Log

The Journal should not say:

```text
QUEST FAILED:
ROOM 14.
```

It records:

```text
WHAT HAPPENED

AND

WHAT THE GROUP
DECIDED.
```

The world determines what follows.

---

# 36. People

The People section records relevant people the group has encountered or learned about.

Information may distinguish between:

```text
KNOWN

OBSERVED

REPORTED

PLAYER THEORY.
```

---

# 37. Player Theory Is Not Canon

If players write:

```text
SHERIFF COLEMAN
KILLED THE WOMAN.
```

the system must not convert this into World Truth.

It may instead record:

```text
PLAYER THEORY:

Coleman may be involved
in the disappearance.
```

The theory may be correct.

It may also be completely wrong.

---

# 38. Evidence and Notes

Players may collect or record:

- photographs
- letters
- documents
- telephone numbers
- names
- license plates
- radio messages
- news articles
- digital files
- observations
- suspicious objects
- personal theories

An item appearing here does not guarantee importance.

---

# 39. Strange Does Not Mean Important

A collected object may be:

```text
CRITICAL

USEFUL

OUTDATED

MISLEADING

MISUNDERSTOOD

OR

IRRELEVANT.
```

The Game Master does not reveal which category applies merely because the group recorded it.

---

# 40. Player Notes

Players should be able to create their own:

```text
NOTES

THEORIES

SUSPICIONS

REMINDERS

MAP MARKERS.
```

The Game Master may help preserve information.

It should not interpret the world for them.

---

# 41. Group Knowledge

The shared Field Menu primarily represents information available to the group.

Conceptually:

```text
WORLD TRUTH

      ↓

CHARACTER A KNOWLEDGE
CHARACTER B KNOWLEDGE
CHARACTER C KNOWLEDGE

      ↓

INFORMATION
SHARED WITH GROUP

      ↓

GROUP RECORD.
```

Private Character knowledge should not automatically appear.

---

# 42. Physical Possession

Information and physical objects follow different rules.

Physical objects exist somewhere in the world.

Core principle:

> **Every physical object exists somewhere.**

---

# 43. No Magical Group Inventory

The group does not possess a dimensionless shared inventory.

Instead, objects may be:

```text
CARRIED BY
A CHARACTER

WORN BY
A CHARACTER

INSIDE
A BACKPACK

STORED IN
A VEHICLE

STORED IN
A BUILDING

LEFT AT
A LOCATION.
```

---

# 44. Plausible Carrying

Core rule:

> **If a Character cannot plausibly carry an object, the Character cannot simply take it with them.**

Carrying depends on more than numerical weight.

Relevant factors include:

```text
WEIGHT

SIZE

SHAPE

DISTANCE

CHARACTER
CONDITION

OTHER
EQUIPMENT

ENVIRONMENT

AVAILABLE
TRANSPORT.
```

---

# 45. Carrying Is Contextual

A Character may reasonably:

```text
CARRY
A CAR BATTERY

FROM A GARAGE
TO A VEHICLE.
```

That does not mean the Character can reasonably:

```text
CARRY
THE SAME BATTERY

FOR 12 KM

THROUGH FOREST

WHILE ALSO
CARRYING
NORMAL EQUIPMENT.
```

The world remains physically plausible.

---

# 46. Avoid Arbitrary Inventory Slots

Project Ascension should not reduce physical logistics to:

```text
YOU HAVE
12 INVENTORY
SLOTS.
```

The Game Master should reason from physical reality.

Mechanical support may later assist this process without replacing plausibility.

---

# 47. Personal Equipment

A Character may carry items such as:

- clothing
- phone
- documents
- water
- food
- medicine
- small tools
- flashlight
- radio
- personal equipment

The exact amount depends on context.

---

# 48. Bags and Backpacks

Containers increase practical carrying capacity.

But they remain physical objects with finite realistic capacity.

A backpack does not remove weight, size or mobility constraints.

---

# 49. Group Carrying Capacity

A larger group can distribute equipment.

Conceptually:

```text
1 CHARACTER
=
LIMITED CAPACITY

3 CHARACTERS
=
GREATER DISTRIBUTION

6 CHARACTERS
=
SIGNIFICANT
COLLECTIVE CAPACITY.
```

This is a natural advantage of group size.

---

# 50. Larger Groups Also Consume More

Greater carrying capacity comes with greater requirements.

More people generally require more:

```text
FOOD

WATER

MEDICINE

SPACE

TRANSPORT

SHELTER.
```

A larger group is therefore not automatically superior.

---

# 51. Vehicles as Logistics

Vehicles provide more than transportation.

They provide:

```text
MOBILITY

+

STORAGE

+

EQUIPMENT
CAPACITY.
```

A vehicle can fundamentally change what the group is capable of carrying.

---

# 52. Vehicle Storage

A vehicle may contain:

- food
- water
- fuel
- tools
- spare parts
- clothing
- blankets
- larger equipment
- collected objects

These objects remain physically inside the vehicle.

---

# 53. Vehicle Dependency

Vehicles also create dependency.

```text
VEHICLE

↓

MORE STORAGE
FASTER TRAVEL
MORE EQUIPMENT

BUT

NEEDS FUEL
NEEDS ROADS
NEEDS MAINTENANCE
CAN BREAK
CAN BE STOLEN
CAN BE SEARCHED
CAN IDENTIFY
THE GROUP.
```

---

# 54. Leaving Equipment Behind

If players leave equipment in a vehicle:

```text
THE EQUIPMENT
STAYS THERE.
```

It does not follow the Characters automatically.

If they later need an item stored three kilometers away:

```text
THEY DO NOT
HAVE IT.
```

---

# 55. Preparation Is Gameplay

Before leaving a location, players may need to decide:

```text
WHAT DO
WE TAKE?

WHAT DO
WE LEAVE?

WHO CARRIES
WHAT?

WHAT STAYS
IN THE CAR?
```

These decisions can create later consequences.

---

# 56. Physical Object State

Conceptually:

```text
WORLD
│
├── LOCATION
│   └── Objects
│
├── BUILDING
│   └── Stored Objects
│
├── VEHICLE
│   └── Transported Objects
│
└── CHARACTER
    ├── Worn
    ├── Carried
    └── Bag / Backpack
```

An object occupies an actual location.

---

# 57. Object Persistence

If an object is left somewhere, it remains there unless:

```text
SOMEONE MOVES IT

IT IS DESTROYED

IT IS LOST

OR

WORLD EVENTS
CHANGE ITS STATE.
```

Objects do not teleport for player convenience.

---

# 58. Character Loss

If a Character disappears, becomes separated, is captured or dies, the group may lose more than that Character's presence.

They may lose access to:

```text
EQUIPMENT

FOOD

MEDICINE

KEYS

DOCUMENTS

PHYSICAL
EVIDENCE

PRIVATE
KNOWLEDGE

EXPERTISE.
```

---

# 59. Information Can Be Lost

If only one Character knows something:

```text
AND

THAT CHARACTER
DISAPPEARS

BEFORE SHARING IT

THEN

THE GROUP MAY
LOSE THAT
KNOWLEDGE.
```

The information may still exist elsewhere in the world.

The group simply no longer possesses it.

---

# 60. Recorded Information Can Survive

If information was:

```text
WRITTEN DOWN

PHOTOGRAPHED

RECORDED

OR

SHARED
```

then others may retain access.

But the physical or digital record must itself remain accessible.

---

# 61. Losing a Vehicle

A lost vehicle can therefore mean:

```text
LOSS OF
TRANSPORT

+

LOSS OF
STORAGE

+

LOSS OF
SUPPLIES

+

LOSS OF
EQUIPMENT

+

LOSS OF
FUTURE OPTIONS.
```

The Game Master does not need to artificially penalize the group.

The physical consequences are already significant.

---

# 62. Travel

Movement between locations is a meaningful Character decision.

Travel may depend on:

- distance
- route
- transportation
- fuel
- road condition
- weather
- information
- security
- time
- physical condition

---

# 63. Travel Is Not Automatic Teleportation

The world exists between destinations.

A journey may contain:

```text
NOTHING IMPORTANT

ORDINARY EVENTS

INFORMATION

HUMAN ENCOUNTERS

DELAYS

OPPORTUNITIES

DANGER

NEW SEEDS.
```

Not every journey requires an encounter.

---

# 64. Time Passes

Actions consume plausible amounts of time.

Examples:

```text
CONVERSATION

SEARCHING

REPAIR

TRAVEL

WAITING

REST

INVESTIGATION.
```

The world continues during that time.

---

# 65. Waiting Is an Action

Players may decide:

> "We wait until morning."

That is a legitimate action.

But waiting advances the world.

Other people and processes may continue.

---

# 66. Leaving Is an Action

Players may leave almost any situation if physically possible.

The Game Master does not require them to complete a narrative objective.

---

# 67. Ignoring Is an Action

Players may hear about something and choose:

```text
NOT OUR
PROBLEM.
```

That decision is valid.

But:

> **Ignored does not mean paused.**

---

# 68. The World Continues

A situation the players ignore may:

```text
RESOLVE

ESCALATE

DISAPPEAR

CHANGE

COLLIDE WITH
ANOTHER SEED

OR

NEVER MATTER
AGAIN.
```

The Game Master follows causality.

---

# 69. No Quest Failure

If players leave a situation:

```text
THEY DID NOT
FAIL THE QUEST.
```

There may never have been a quest.

They made a decision.

The world responded.

---

# 70. Player Freedom

At any given moment, players may attempt something the Game Master did not anticipate.

That is expected.

The system should support:

```text
PLAYER INTENT

↓

GAME MASTER
INTERPRETATION

↓

WORLD
CONSTRAINTS

↓

RESOLUTION
WHEN NEEDED

↓

CONSEQUENCE.
```

---

# 71. Resolution

When an intended action has meaningful uncertainty, the Game Master invokes the Action and Resolution System.

Not every action requires a roll.

The player says:

```text
"I TRY."
```

The world answers.

---

# 72. Consequence

Every meaningful action may change:

- World State
- Character State
- Relationships
- Knowledge
- resources
- physical possession
- Seeds
- future options

Consequences become part of history.

---

---

# 77. The World Does Not Exist for the Players

The world contains:

- people the players never meet
- places they never visit
- Seeds they never discover
- events they never understand
- objects they never find
- information they never receive

This is intentional.

---

# 78. The Interface Must Not Reveal Importance

The UI must not automatically tell players:

```text
THIS PERSON
MATTERS.

THIS OBJECT
IS A CLUE.

THIS LOCATION
CONTAINS
CONTENT.

THIS RUMOR
IS TRUE.
```

Players construct significance through experience.

---

# 79. Ordinary Life Matters

The world must contain:

- ordinary people
- ordinary conversations
- normal buildings
- uneventful travel
- meals
- waiting
- jokes
- disagreements
- boredom
- comfort

Without ordinary life, danger loses meaning.

---

# 80. Nothing Happening Is Valid

Sometimes players investigate something and discover:

```text
NOTHING
UNUSUAL.
```

Sometimes the strange man at the gas station is simply strange.

Sometimes the suspicious car belongs to someone visiting family.

Not every uncertainty hides a Seed.

---

# 81. Game Master Restraint

The Game Master must resist the urge to constantly create activity.

It should allow:

```text
SPACE

SILENCE

UNCERTAINTY

PLAYER
DISCUSSION

ORDINARY
MOMENTS.
```

The players do not need continuous stimulation.

---

# 82. Shared Memory of the Adventure

Over time:

```text
MAP

JOURNAL

PEOPLE

NOTES

EVIDENCE

OBJECTS

RELATIONSHIPS

MEMORIES
```

form a record of the group's unique history.

Another group playing from the same starting conditions may create a completely different history.

---

# 83. The Adventure Is Not Prewritten

The Game Master begins with:

```text
WORLD STATE

CHARACTERS

RELATIONSHIPS

HISTORY

SEEDS

PRESSURES

POSSIBILITIES.
```

It does not begin with a predetermined sequence of scenes the players must complete.

---

# 84. Player Actions Create History

The campaign gradually becomes:

```text
WHAT THE
WORLD DID

+

WHAT THE
PLAYERS DID

+
```

```text
WHAT THE
```

The players decide whether to engage.

The Game Master Loop continuously determines:

```text
WHAT IS
HAPPENING

RELEVANT
```

Player Experience defines how that process reaches the human players.

# 87. Relationship to Action and Resolution

Player Experience provides:

```text
INTENT.
```

Action and Resolution determines uncertain outcome.

World Simulation receives the consequence.

# 88. Relationship to World Simulation

World Simulation owns:

```text
WHAT IS TRUE.
```

Player Experience owns neither World Truth nor hidden state.

It presents only what Characters can plausibly experience.

# 89. Primary Design Test

When evaluating a future gameplay feature, ask:

> **Does this help the players experience and understand their place in the world, or does it make the game behave more like a conventional quest interface?**

If the feature unnecessarily tells players:

```text
WHERE TO GO

WHAT MATTERS

WHAT IS TRUE

WHAT THEY
SHOULD DO
```

it should be treated with suspicion.

---

# 90. Living Room Test

A second design test is:

> **Does this work when several friends are sitting together in a darkened living room looking at one large television?**

The experience should encourage them to:

```text
LOOK

LISTEN

DISCUSS

ARGUE

SPECULATE

REMEMBER

PLAN

DECIDE.
```

Not merely wait for their individual turn to press a button.

# 92. Closing Principle

Project Ascension does not ask the players to find the story.

It gives them a world.

They arrive somewhere.

They see what their Characters can see.

They hear what their Characters can hear.

They carry only what they can actually bring with them.

They remember some things.

They forget others.

They collect photographs, names, rumors, food, tools, keys and questions.

They meet people they may never see again.

They leave places before understanding them.

They make decisions with incomplete information.

Sometimes they return.

Sometimes they cannot.

The map records where they have been.

The Journal records what happened.

Their possessions record what they chose to carry.

Their Relationships record who they affected.

Their Characters remember what they experienced.

And the world remembers what they did.

The Game Master does not tell them which road contains the adventure.

The players choose a road.

The world decides what is there.

> **The world contains truth. The Characters possess information. The players build understanding.**

> **The Game Master presents the world. The players decide what matters.**
