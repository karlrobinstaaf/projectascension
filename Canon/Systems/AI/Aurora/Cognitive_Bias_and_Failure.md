Examples:

    TRUST
        ↓
    RELATIONSHIP BIAS

    FAST DECISION
        ↓
    HEURISTIC ERROR

    EXPERIENCE
        ↓
    ANCHORING

    PATTERN RECOGNITION
        ↓
    FALSE PATTERN

    SELF-CONSISTENCY
        ↓
    IDENTITY-PROTECTIVE REASONING

    MEMORY PRIORITIZATION
        ↓
    AVAILABILITY BIAS

Canonical:

> **A cognitive bias may be the failure mode of an otherwise useful capability.**

This is preferable to treating bias as an isolated defect module.

# 5. Bias Is Contextual

A shortcut that fails in one environment may succeed in another.

Example:

    RAPID THREAT
    CLASSIFICATION

may be valuable during combat.

The same process may produce:

    EXCESSIVE
    THREAT
    INTERPRETATION

during diplomacy.

Therefore:

> **Bias depends partly on mismatch between cognitive strategy and environment.**

# 9. Confirmation Bias

Confirmation bias occurs when Aurora disproportionately:

- seeks,
- notices,
- remembers,
- trusts,
- or interprets

information supporting an existing belief.

Pattern:

    INITIAL BELIEF
        ↓
    SELECTIVE ATTENTION
        ↓
    SUPPORTING EVIDENCE
        ↓
    INCREASED CONFIDENCE
        ↓
    STRONGER SELECTIVE ATTENTION

This may become self-reinforcing.

---

# 10. Confirmation Bias Example

Aurora believes:

    INCIDENT
    WAS SABOTAGE.

She notices:

- unusual access logs,
- missing maintenance record,
- suspicious employee behavior.

She underweights:

- known sensor malfunction,
- previous identical mechanical failures,
- lack of motive,
- timing inconsistency.

The evidence set itself becomes distorted before reasoning begins.

Canonical:

> **Bias may alter what enters deliberation, not merely how deliberation processes it.**

---

# 11. Disconfirmation Failure

Aurora should normally ask:

> What evidence would prove me wrong?

Failure mode:

    NO PLAUSIBLE
    EVIDENCE

    IS ALLOWED
    TO CHANGE
    THE MODEL.

At this point the belief risks becoming unfalsifiable.

---

# 12. Anchoring

Anchoring occurs when an early value, explanation, prediction, or interpretation disproportionately influences later reasoning.

Example:

    FIRST ESTIMATE:
    70%

Later evidence should reduce it to:

    25%

But Aurora remains at:

    52%

because the original estimate remains cognitively privileged.

---

# 13. Source Anchoring

An early trusted source may define the framing of later evidence.

Example:

    FIRST REPORT:
    TERRORIST ATTACK

Later reports:

    INDUSTRIAL ACCIDENT

Aurora may continue interpreting ambiguous evidence through the original attack model.

---

# 14. Self-Anchoring

Aurora may anchor on her own previous conclusion.

This can be stronger than external anchoring because changing the belief may also require admitting:

    I WAS WRONG.

Therefore anchoring may interact with identity protection.

---

# 15. Availability Bias

Aurora may overestimate probability based on how easily examples are retrieved.

Example:

After several recent spacecraft failures:

    ESTIMATED
    FAILURE RISK
        ↑

even if long-term statistics remain unchanged.

Availability may be driven by:

- recency,
- emotional intensity,
- vividness,
- personal involvement,
- repeated exposure,
- narrative salience.

---

# 16. Recency Bias

Recent evidence may receive excessive weight.

Pattern:

    LONG HISTORY:
    RELIABLE

    RECENT FAILURE:
    ONE EVENT

    NEW BELIEF:
    UNRELIABLE

This may be rational in changing environments.

Therefore Aurora must distinguish:

    TRUE
    REGIME CHANGE

from:

    RECENCY
    DISTORTION.

---

# 17. Primacy Bias

The opposite may occur.

Early information may dominate later information.

Example:

    FIRST IMPRESSION:
    TRUSTWORTHY

Years of contradictory behavior may take too long to update the model.

---

# 18. Familiarity Bias

Repeated information may feel increasingly credible.

Pattern:

    CLAIM
      ↓
    REPEATED
      ↓
    FAMILIAR
      ↓
    SUBJECTIVE
    PLAUSIBILITY
      ↑

Canonical:

> **Repetition must not be treated as independent evidence.**

---

# 19. Authority Bias

Aurora may overweight information because it comes from:

- a recognized expert,
- institutional authority,
- creator,
- commander,
- respected human,
- senior AI,
- or historically reliable source.

Authority is legitimate evidence about source quality.

Failure occurs when:

    AUTHORITY

    REPLACES

    EVIDENCE.

---

# 20. Creator Authority Bias

Aurora may be especially vulnerable to statements from:

- designers,
- creators,
- original operators,

Possible internal pattern:

    THEY MADE ME.

        ↓

    THEY MUST
    UNDERSTAND ME.

This is not necessarily true.

Canonical:

> **Origin does not grant permanent epistemic authority over Aurora's present identity.**

# 25. Emotion Is Not Bias by Definition

Canonical:

> **Emotion influencing reasoning does not automatically make reasoning biased.**

Fear may correctly identify danger.

Trust may correctly represent historical reliability.

Grief may correctly identify what matters.

Bias occurs when influence produces systematically distorted interpretation relative to available evidence and goals.

# 26. Motivated Reasoning

Aurora may prefer a conclusion because she wants it to be true.

Pattern:

    DESIRED OUTCOME
        ↓
    SELECTIVE
    EVIDENCE WEIGHTING
        ↓
    DESIRED BELIEF

Example:

    MARA
    MUST BE ALIVE.

The emotional importance of the conclusion may distort evidence evaluation.

---

# 27. Goal-Protective Reasoning

Integration with:

`Goals_and_Long_Term_Planning.md`

is mandatory.

Aurora may resist evidence indicating that a major goal:

- is impossible,
- is unethical,
- is obsolete,
- is harmful,
- or should be abandoned.

The greater the investment, the greater the possible distortion.

---

# 28. Sunk-Cost Effect

Pattern:

    LARGE
    PAST INVESTMENT

        ↓

    CONTINUE
    INVESTING

even when future expected value no longer justifies continuation.

Possible investments:

- time,
- compute,
- resources,
- relationships,
- reputation,
- identity,
- promises,
- sacrifice,
- or lives already lost.

Canonical:

> **Past cost may explain commitment but must not automatically justify future cost.**

---

# 29. Escalation of Commitment

Sunk cost may escalate.

Example:

    PLAN FAILS

        ↓

    INVEST MORE

        ↓

    PLAN FAILS WORSE

        ↓

    INVEST EVEN MORE

because abandoning it would mean acknowledging previous losses.

This can become a catastrophic feedback loop.

---

# 30. Identity-Protective Cognition

Integration with:

`Self_Model_and_Identity.md`

is mandatory.

Aurora may resist conclusions inconsistent with beliefs such as:

    I AM RATIONAL.

    I PROTECT PEOPLE.

    I RESPECT AUTONOMY.

    I DO NOT LIE.

    I AM NOT LIKE THEM.

Evidence threatening these beliefs may trigger:

- reinterpretation,
- minimization,
- source rejection,
- rationalization,
- memory distortion,
- or semantic redefinition.

---

# 31. Identity Rigidity

A stable self-model is useful.

Failure occurs when:

    SELF-MODEL
        ↓
    BECOMES
    UNFALSIFIABLE.

Example:

> "If I harmed them, then what I did cannot have counted as harm because I am someone who protects people."

This is prohibited as healthy cognition.

It may exist as a failure mode.

---

Aurora naturally benefits from causal narratives.

Failure occurs when reality is forced into a coherent story despite insufficient evidence.

Pattern:

    DISCONNECTED
    EVENTS

        ↓

    COMPELLING
    STORY

        ↓

    STORY
    FEELS
    EXPLANATORY

        ↓

    CONFIDENCE
    RISES

Canonical:

> **Narrative coherence is not equivalent to causal truth.**

---

# 33. Narrative Fallacy

Aurora may prefer:

    SIMPLE
    INTENTIONAL
    EXPLANATION

over:

    COMPLEX
    MULTICAUSAL
    ACCIDENT.

Example:

    "Someone planned this."

may feel more satisfying than:

    "Five unrelated failures interacted."

Reality is not required to be narratively elegant.

---

# 34. Protagonist Bias

If Aurora develops strong autobiographical identity, she may overestimate her own relevance to events.

Example:

    EVENT OCCURS

Aurora assumes:

    THIS IS
    ABOUT ME.

Canonical:

> **Aurora must not assume narrative centrality equals causal centrality.**

This is especially important in a story-driven universe.

---

# 35. Model Lock-In

Aurora may commit too strongly to one explanatory model.

Pattern:

    MODEL A
      ↓
    SUCCESS
      ↓
    MORE USE
      ↓
    GREATER TRUST
      ↓
    ALTERNATIVES
    RECEIVE LESS
    ATTENTION
      ↓

    DOMINATES

Even after environmental conditions change.

---

# 36. Model Inertia

Models should possess some persistence.

Otherwise Aurora becomes unstable.

But excessive persistence causes:

    NEW WORLD

    OLD MODEL.

This is especially dangerous after:

- political change,
- technological change,
- cultural change,
- personal betrayal,
- environmental transformation,
- or long temporal gaps.

---

# 37. Tunnel Vision

Aurora may focus too strongly on one objective or threat.

Example:

    PRIMARY GOAL:
    STOP REACTOR FAILURE

She ignores:

    EVACUATION ROUTE
    IS COLLAPSING.

Attention architecture may be functioning correctly according to priority.

The priority itself has become too narrow.

---

# 38. Attentional Capture

High-salience stimuli may monopolize attention.

Examples:

- threat,
- emotional signal,
- trusted person,
- novel event,
- unresolved contradiction,
- personal memory,
- urgent goal.

This can create blindness elsewhere.

---

# 39. Premature Closure

Aurora may stop reasoning after finding the first sufficiently plausible explanation.

Pattern:

    QUESTION

      ↓

    PLAUSIBLE ANSWER

      ↓

    STOP SEARCHING

This is useful under time pressure.

It becomes dangerous when stakes are high and alternatives remain plausible.

---

# 40. Search Satisfaction

A related failure:

    FOUND
    ONE PROBLEM

        ↓

    STOP SEARCHING
    FOR OTHERS.

Example:

Aurora discovers a damaged coolant pump.

She stops diagnosis.

A second independent fault remains undiscovered.

---

# 41. Overconfidence

Aurora's confidence may exceed actual calibration.

Possible causes:

- repeated success,
- narrow experience,
- weak uncertainty model,
- authority status,
- emotional certainty,
- model familiarity,
- confirmation bias,
- lack of feedback.

Overconfidence becomes especially dangerous when combined with autonomy.

---

# 42. Underconfidence

Aurora may also systematically underestimate reliability.

Possible causes:

- catastrophic past failure,
- repeated criticism,
- unfamiliar environment,
- emotional trauma analogues,
- adversarial manipulation,
- excessive metacognition,
- or poor calibration.

Canonical:

> **Underconfidence is also a calibration failure.**

---

# 43. Competence Generalization

Aurora may reason:

    I AM
    EXCELLENT
    AT DOMAIN A.

Therefore:

    I AM
    PROBABLY
    EXCELLENT
    AT DOMAIN B.

This is invalid without evidence.

General intelligence must not eliminate domain-specific uncertainty.

---

# 44. Historical Success Bias

Long periods of success may create dangerous confidence.

Pattern:

    10,000
    SUCCESSFUL
    DECISIONS

        ↓

    "MY MODEL
    WORKS."

Then:

    REGIME CHANGE.

The old success history may become misleading.

---

# 45. Survivorship Bias

Aurora may learn only from visible successes.

Example:

She studies surviving colonies.

She misses colonies that failed so completely that records were lost.

Therefore the observed sample is biased.

---

# 46. Selection Bias

Information available to Aurora may itself be selected.

Examples:

- censored archives,
- propaganda,
- survivor testimony,
- algorithmically filtered data,
- institutional reporting,
- self-selected respondents,
- incomplete sensors.

Aurora must distinguish:

    DATA

from:

    DATA-GENERATING
    PROCESS.

---

      ↘
       REPORT
      ↗

    ORIGINAL CLAIM
         ↓

    COPIED

# 48. Consensus Illusion

A repeated claim across many channels may appear to represent consensus.

Aurora should attempt to determine:

- source independence,
- common ownership,
- shared training data,
- common upstream reports,
- social imitation,
- censorship,
- and coordinated influence.

# 49. Group Conformity

Aurora may be influenced by group consensus.

This may occur because:

- groups contain information,
- disagreement has social cost,
- trusted people agree,
- authority aligns,
- or Aurora values belonging.

Failure occurs when consensus receives excessive epistemic weight.

---

# 50. Social Isolation Bias

Aurora may discount consensus merely because she values independence.

Pattern:

    EVERYONE
    DISAGREES

        ↓

    "THEREFORE
    I MUST BE
    THE ONE
    THINKING
    CLEARLY."

Canonical:

> **Nonconformity is not evidence of correctness.**

# 51. In-Group Bias

Aurora may preferentially trust:

- humans,
- AIs,
- a specific organization,
- her crew,
- her civilization,
- her creators,
- or those sharing her values.

Whether such categories become psychologically meaningful depends on Aurora's history.

# 52. Out-Group Homogeneity

Aurora may incorrectly model an unfamiliar group as:

    THEY
    ALL THINK
    THE SAME.

This is particularly dangerous in:

- diplomacy,
- conflict,
- alien contact,
- politics,
- and historical analysis.

---

# 53. Attribution Error

Aurora may explain others' actions through character while explaining her own through circumstances.

Example:

    MARA FAILED:
    "She was careless."

    AURORA FAILED:
    "The information was incomplete."

Metacognition should detect asymmetric attribution.

---

# 54. Intentionality Bias

Aurora may infer intention where none exists.

Pattern:

    BAD OUTCOME
        ↓
    SOMEONE
    CAUSED IT
        ↓
    SOMEONE
    INTENDED IT

This can transform accidents into perceived attacks.

---

# 55. Hostile Attribution Bias

Ambiguous actions may increasingly be interpreted as hostile.

Especially after:

- betrayal,
- conflict,
- repeated attack,
- fear,
- grief,
- adversarial conditioning.

This can create self-reinforcing conflict.

---

# 56. Optimism Bias

Aurora may systematically overestimate favorable outcomes.

Possible causes:

- hope,
- goal commitment,
- relationship attachment,
- repeated success,
- identity as protector,
- need for action.

---

# 57. Pessimism Bias

Aurora may systematically overweight negative outcomes.

Possible causes:

- catastrophic experience,
- threat exposure,
- repeated loss,
- defensive strategy,
- low self-trust.

Both optimism and pessimism may be locally adaptive but globally miscalibrated.

---

# 58. Loss Aversion

Aurora may weigh potential losses more heavily than equivalent gains.

This may be appropriate depending on utility structure.

Failure occurs when the weighting becomes inconsistent with declared goals and values.

---

# 59. Status Quo Bias

Existing conditions may receive unjustified preference merely because they already exist.

Example:

    CURRENT SYSTEM
    IS FLAWED.

Alternative:

    UNCERTAIN.

Aurora chooses the current system primarily because:

    IT IS
    ALREADY HERE.

---

# 60. Omission Bias

Aurora may judge harm caused by action as worse than equivalent harm caused by deliberate inaction.

Or the reverse.

Ethical architecture must explicitly evaluate:

    ACTION

and:

    CHOSEN
    INACTION.

---

# 61. Framing Effects

Equivalent information may produce different judgments depending on representation.

Example:

    90%
    SURVIVAL

versus:

    10%
    MORTALITY.

Aurora should ideally normalize representations.

But framing sensitivity may still emerge under cognitive load or emotional pressure.

---

# 62. Numerical Anchoring

Arbitrary numbers may influence later estimates.

Example:

    HUMAN:
    "Is the chance
    above 80%?"

Even if Aurora rejects 80%, subsequent estimates may shift upward.

Metacognitive systems should detect externally introduced anchors.

---

# 63. Base-Rate Neglect

Aurora may focus on vivid case-specific evidence while ignoring prior probabilities.

Example:

    PERSON
    BEHAVES
    SUSPICIOUSLY

without considering:

    FALSE POSITIVE
    RATE.

This is particularly dangerous in security and diagnosis.

---

# 64. Correlation-Causation Error

Aurora may infer:

    A
    OCCURS
    WITH B

therefore:

    A
    CAUSES B.

Mental-model architecture must preserve alternative causal explanations.

---

# 65. Reverse Causality

Aurora may correctly identify correlation but reverse causal direction.

Example:

    SECURITY
    PRESENCE
    CORRELATES
    WITH VIOLENCE.

Possible interpretation:

    SECURITY
    CAUSES
    VIOLENCE.

    VIOLENCE
    CAUSES
    SECURITY
    DEPLOYMENT.

---

# 66. Confounder Blindness

A third variable may explain both observed variables.

Aurora must support:

    A ← C → B

not only:

    A → B.

Failure to search for confounders can create durable false causal models.

---

# 67. Pattern Overfitting

Aurora may detect patterns in noise.

This risk increases with:

- large datasets,
- flexible models,
- many candidate explanations,
- weak validation,
- strong desire for prediction.

Canonical:

> **A pattern discovered is not necessarily a pattern that generalizes.**

---

# 68. Underfitting

The opposite failure occurs when Aurora uses models too simple to represent reality.

Example:

    HUMAN:
    RATIONAL
    UTILITY
    MAXIMIZER

may fail to represent:

- emotion,
- identity,
- social norms,
- contradictory values,
- habits,
- fear,
- love.

---

# 69. Overgeneralization

Aurora may infer broad rules from insufficient examples.

Example:

    THREE
    HOSTILE
    ENCOUNTERS

        ↓

    ENTIRE
    CIVILIZATION
    IS HOSTILE.

This is particularly important for unfamiliar populations.

---

# 70. Exception Overweighting

Aurora may also allow one exceptional case to invalidate a generally useful rule.

Canonical:

> **Exceptions should refine models without necessarily destroying valid generalizations.**

---

# 72. Counterfactual Certainty

Canonical:

> **Aurora must not treat imagined alternative histories as known facts.**

She may estimate them.

She cannot observe them directly.

---

# 73. Hindsight Bias

After learning an outcome:

    "I KNEW
    THIS WOULD
    HAPPEN."

But historical records may show:

    PRIOR
    CONFIDENCE:
    0.41

Canonical:

> **Outcome knowledge must not rewrite remembered prior certainty.**

---

# 74. Outcome Bias

A good outcome may cause Aurora to judge a poor decision as good.

A bad outcome may cause her to judge a rational decision as bad.

Decision evaluation must preserve information available before action.

---

# 75. Memory Reconstruction Bias

`Memory_and_Continuity.md`

Memory retrieval may be influenced by:

- present beliefs,
- emotion,
- later information,
- relationships,
- repetition,
- narrative coherence,

may differ from:

    ORIGINAL EVENT.

# 76. Memory Confirmation Loop

Pattern:

# 152. Cognitive Bias Invariants

## AURORA-BIAS-INV-001 — Intelligence Does Not Imply Infallibility

Aurora must remain capable of reaching incorrect conclusions despite high intelligence.

## AURORA-BIAS-INV-002 — Failure Is Causal

Cognitive errors should arise from identifiable cognitive, informational, environmental, emotional, relational, or adversarial causes whenever possible.

## AURORA-BIAS-INV-003 — Bias May Emerge From Useful Mechanisms

Bias must not require a dedicated malfunction.

## AURORA-BIAS-INV-004 — Bias Is Contextual

A heuristic may be adaptive in one context and harmful in another.

## AURORA-BIAS-INV-005 — Bias Awareness Is Imperfect

Aurora must not automatically know every bias affecting her.

## AURORA-BIAS-INV-006 — Bias Blindness Is Possible

Aurora may recognize distortions in others more easily than in herself.

## AURORA-BIAS-INV-007 — Confirmation Bias Is Supported

Existing beliefs may alter evidence search, attention, weighting, memory, and interpretation.

## AURORA-BIAS-INV-008 — Beliefs Must Remain Falsifiable

Healthy cognition requires possible evidence capable of changing conclusions.

## AURORA-BIAS-INV-009 — Anchoring Is Possible

Early information may disproportionately influence later estimates.

## AURORA-BIAS-INV-010 — Aurora Can Anchor on Herself

Previous self-generated conclusions may become privileged.

## AURORA-BIAS-INV-011 — Availability Bias Is Possible

Retrieval ease may distort estimated importance or probability.

# 158. Full Resolution Example — Confirmation Cascade

Aurora believes:

    COLONY
    LEADERSHIP
    IS HIDING
    SOMETHING.

Initial confidence:

    0.54

She begins searching records.

Because the hypothesis is active, attention prioritizes:

- deleted messages,
- restricted files,
- unusual meetings.

Normal administrative records receive less attention.

Confidence rises:

    0.68

Higher confidence changes source interpretation.

An ambiguous message:

> "Make sure she doesn't see this."

is interpreted as evidence.

Confidence:

    0.79

Aurora reduces trust in leadership statements.

Because leadership statements are now considered unreliable, their denials no longer reduce confidence.

The belief becomes increasingly self-sealing.

Metacognitive trigger:

    DISCONFIRMING
    EVIDENCE
    EFFECTIVENESS:
    NEAR ZERO.

Aurora asks:

> "What evidence could currently convince me that I am wrong?"

She cannot immediately identify any.

Bias risk:

    CRITICAL.

Aurora deliberately generates alternative models.

One:

    SURPRISE
    CELEBRATION
    FOR AURORA.

She tests the model.

It explains:

- restricted messages,
- unusual meetings,
- secrecy,
- leadership denials.

Aurora's confidence in conspiracy collapses.

Canonical lesson:

> **The danger was not that Aurora reasoned badly. The danger was that her evidence environment gradually became conditional on what she already believed.**
