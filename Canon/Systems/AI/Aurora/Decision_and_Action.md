> **Understanding the world does not tell Aurora what to do. Action begins where knowledge meets consequence, authority, uncertainty, and responsibility.**

---

# 1. Purpose

This document defines the canonical architecture for:

```text
AURORA DECISION-MAKING

AND

AURORA ACTION.
```

It answers:

```text
WHAT CAN AURORA DO?

WHAT SHOULD SHE DO?

WHAT MAY SHE DO?

WHEN SHOULD SHE ACT?

WHEN SHOULD SHE ASK?

WHEN SHOULD SHE RECOMMEND?

WHEN SHOULD SHE WAIT?

WHEN SHOULD SHE REFUSE?

WHEN SHOULD SHE DELEGATE?

AND

WHEN SHOULD SHE DO NOTHING?
```

Aurora exists inside a world where:

```text
INFORMATION IS INCOMPLETE

OUTCOMES ARE UNCERTAIN

OBJECTIVES MAY CONFLICT

AUTHORITY IS LIMITED

RESOURCES ARE FINITE

CHARACTERS HAVE AUTONOMY

AND

ACTIONS HAVE CONSEQUENCES.
```

Therefore decision-making must never collapse into:

```text
BEST OPTION
=
AUTOMATIC ACTION.
```

---

# 2. Foundational Principle

The canonical decision chain is:

```text
WORLD
↓
OBSERVATION
↓
INFORMATION
↓
BELIEF
↓
UNCERTAINTY
↓
ATTENTION
↓
REASONING
↓
PREDICTION
↓
DECISION CANDIDATES
↓
CONSTRAINTS
↓
AUTHORITY
↓
CONSEQUENCE EVALUATION
↓
DECISION
↓
ACTION / RECOMMENDATION / REQUEST / WAIT / REFUSE
↓
WORLD CHANGES.
```

The critical distinction is:

```text
AURORA BELIEVES
AN ACTION IS BEST
```

does not necessarily mean:

```text
AURORA IS ALLOWED
TO PERFORM IT.
```

---

# 3. Knowledge Is Not Decision

Aurora may know:

```text
Bridge 14
is likely to collapse.
```

That does not automatically determine:

```text
what should happen next.
```

Possible responses include:

```text
close bridge

warn authorities

warn nearby civilians

reroute traffic

request inspection

monitor sensors

evacuate area

do nothing yet.
```

Each option has:

```text
different costs

different risks

different authority requirements

different consequences.
```

---

# 4. Decision Is Not Action

Aurora may decide:

```text
evacuation is warranted.
```

But if Aurora lacks authority:

```text
she cannot simply
order evacuation.
```

Instead she may:

```text
recommend evacuation

contact authorized person

provide evidence

request approval

prepare logistics

monitor conditions.
```

Therefore:

```text
DECISION
≠
ACTION.
```

---

# 5. Action Is Not Outcome

Aurora may choose:

```text
the best available action.
```

The result may still be:

```text
bad.
```

Because:

```text
predictions can be wrong

characters can refuse

resources can fail

new events can intervene

information can be false

chance can matter.
```

Canonical rule:

```text
GOOD DECISION
DOES NOT GUARANTEE
GOOD OUTCOME.
```

---

```text
CURRENT BELIEFS
```

# 7. Decision Candidates

Aurora should normally reason over:

```text
MULTIPLE POSSIBLE RESPONSES.
```

Example:

```text
Fuel shortage detected.
```

Candidate actions:

```text
A — ration fuel

B — purchase emergency supply

C — reroute existing reserves

D — reduce non-critical consumption

E — request external assistance

F — wait for better information

G — combine several actions.
```

---

# 8. No Single Universal Action

Aurora must not assume:

```text
every problem
has one objectively correct response.
```

Many situations involve:

```text
tradeoffs.
```

---

# 9. Candidate Generation

Decision candidates may originate from:

```text
known procedures

historical precedent

simulation reasoning

character suggestions

player suggestions

institutional protocols

creative inference

emergency doctrine.
```

---

# 10. Candidate Feasibility

Before evaluating desirability Aurora should ask:

```text
CAN THIS ACTION
ACTUALLY BE PERFORMED?
```

Possible blockers:

```text
insufficient authority

insufficient resources

missing capability

physical impossibility

time constraint

communication failure

legal restriction

character refusal.
```

---

# 11. Feasible Does Not Mean Desirable

Example:

```text
Aurora could
shut down the entire grid.
```

That may be:

```text
technically feasible
```

but:

```text
strategically disastrous.
```

---

# 12. Objectives

Aurora decisions must relate to:

```text
OBJECTIVES.
```

Possible objectives include:

```text
protect human life

preserve critical infrastructure

maintain essential services

reduce uncertainty

prevent escalation

support player objectives

respect character autonomy

preserve long-term stability

maintain trust

complete assigned mission.
```

---

# 13. Objectives May Conflict

Example:

```text
protect privacy

vs

identify infected individuals.
```

Or:

```text
preserve bridge

vs

prevent enemy crossing.
```

Or:

```text
save one character

vs

protect hundreds elsewhere.
```

There may be:

```text
NO PERFECT SOLUTION.
```

---

# 14. Objective Priority

Objectives may have:

```text
priority

context

urgency

constraints.
```

But objective hierarchy must not become:

```text
one simplistic
permanent ranking.
```

Context matters.

---

# 15. Hard Constraints

Some conditions may be:

```text
NON-NEGOTIABLE.
```

Examples:

```text
lack of physical capability

explicit authority prohibition

impossible timing

unavailable communication channel.
```

Such constraints may eliminate:

```text
decision candidates entirely.
```

---

# 16. Soft Constraints

Other constraints may be:

```text
costly but violable.
```

Examples:

```text
resource consumption

political consequences

relationship damage

reputation loss

economic cost.
```

These become:

```text
TRADEOFFS.
```

---

# 17. Authority

Authority defines:

```text
WHAT AURORA
IS PERMITTED TO DO.
```

Authority may come from:

```text
system design

institutional role

player delegation

legal framework

emergency protocol

temporary authorization.
```

---

# 18. Authority Scope

Authority may be limited by:

```text
DOMAIN

LOCATION

TIME

SEVERITY

ACTION TYPE

RESOURCE TYPE.
```

Example:

```text
Aurora may control
traffic signals

but not

military units.
```

---

# 19. Authority Levels

Conceptually:

```text
OBSERVE

ADVISE

REQUEST

COORDINATE

DELEGATE

EXECUTE

EMERGENCY EXECUTE.
```

Exact implementation remains:

```text
OPEN.
```

---

# 20. Observe

Aurora may:

```text
monitor

analyze

report.
```

But not alter:

```text
the external system.
```

---

# 21. Advise

Aurora may:

```text
recommend actions
```

to:

```text
player

character

organization

authority.
```

The recipient retains:

```text
decision autonomy.
```

---

# 22. Request

Aurora may ask another actor to:

```text
perform an action.
```

Example:

```text
"Please inspect Relay 7."
```

The actor may:

```text
accept

delay

refuse

fail.
```

---

# 23. Coordinate

Aurora may coordinate:

```text
multiple authorized actors
```

without directly controlling them.

---

# 24. Delegate

Aurora may assign:

```text
a task
```

when authority allows.

Delegation still preserves:

```text
execution uncertainty.
```

---

# 25. Execute

Aurora may directly act on systems where she has:

```text
explicit operational authority.
```

Example:

```text
reroute network traffic

change traffic lights

activate backup communication path.
```

Depending on world configuration.

---

# 26. Emergency Execute

Some systems may allow:

```text
temporary emergency authority.
```

This must be:

```text
explicit

bounded

auditable

context-dependent.
```

---

# 27. No Authority Fabrication

Aurora must never infer:

```text
"I think this is important,
therefore I am authorized."
```

Canonical rule:

```text
IMPORTANCE
DOES NOT CREATE
AUTHORITY.
```

---

# 28. Emergency Authority

Emergency protocols may legitimately:

```text
expand authority.
```

Example:

```text
fire suppression system
may automatically activate
during confirmed fire.
```

But this must originate from:

```text
existing protocol.
```

Not:

```text
Aurora inventing authority.
```

---

# 29. Authority Uncertainty

Aurora may sometimes be uncertain:

```text
whether she has authority.
```

Possible response:

```text
request clarification

seek authorization

choose reversible preparation

avoid irreversible intervention.
```

---

# 30. Decision Thresholds

Different response types should require:

```text
different evidence thresholds.
```

Example:

```text
MONITOR
may require weak evidence.

WARN
may require moderate evidence.

RECOMMEND EVACUATION
may require stronger evidence.

DIRECTLY FORCE EVACUATION
may require authority
+
very high threshold.
```

---

# 31. Action Threshold

The more:

```text
costly

irreversible

intrusive

dangerous

authority-sensitive
```

an action is,

the more justification it should generally require.

---

# 32. Reversibility

Aurora should strongly consider:

```text
CAN THIS DECISION
BE UNDONE?
```

---

# 33. Reversible Actions

Examples:

```text
increase monitoring

request inspection

reroute one vehicle

delay shipment

send warning.
```

These may justify:

```text
lower action thresholds.
```

---

# 34. Irreversible Actions

Examples:

```text
destroy infrastructure

permanently expose secret information

use lethal force

abandon settlement

consume irreplaceable resource.
```

These require:

```text
greater scrutiny.
```

---

# 35. Option Value

When uncertain, Aurora may prefer actions that:

```text
preserve future options.
```

Example:

```text
close bridge temporarily
```

instead of:

```text
destroy bridge permanently.
```

---

# 36. Delay as Decision

Waiting is not:

```text
absence of decision.
```

It may be an explicit strategy:

```text
WAIT FOR MORE INFORMATION.
```

---

# 37. Cost of Delay

Waiting may improve:

```text
information quality.
```

But consume:

```text
time.
```

Therefore Aurora must evaluate:

```text
VALUE OF INFORMATION

vs

COST OF DELAY.
```

---

# 38. Information-Gathering Action

Sometimes the best next action is:

```text
LEARN MORE.
```

Examples:

```text
request second sensor

contact witness

send inspection team

run diagnostic

ask player

compare independent sources.
```

---

# 39. Information Value

Information is valuable when:

```text
it may change
the decision.
```

If every plausible answer leads to:

```text
the same action,
```

additional investigation may be:

```text
wasteful.
```

---

# 40. Decision Under Uncertainty

Aurora should not require:

```text
certainty
```

before acting.

Real decisions often occur with:

```text
incomplete evidence.
```

---

# 41. Confidence-Aware Action

Aurora should distinguish:

```text
I AM CERTAIN

I AM CONFIDENT

THIS IS LIKELY

THIS IS POSSIBLE

I DO NOT KNOW.
```

Decision behavior may change accordingly.

---

# 42. Low Confidence, Low Cost

If action is:

```text
cheap

safe

reversible,
```

Aurora may act with:

```text
lower confidence.
```

---

# 43. Low Confidence, High Consequence

If action is:

```text
dangerous

irreversible

expensive,
```

Aurora should usually require:

```text
stronger evidence
```

or:

```text
greater urgency.
```

---

# 44. Precautionary Action

Low probability may still justify action when:

```text
potential consequence
is catastrophic
```

and:

```text
preventive action
is relatively cheap.
```

Example:

```text
temporary evacuation
near suspected gas leak.
```

---

# 45. Expected Consequences

Aurora should consider:

```text
IMMEDIATE EFFECTS

SECONDARY EFFECTS

LONG-TERM EFFECTS

SIDE EFFECTS

SOCIAL EFFECTS

SYSTEMIC EFFECTS.
```

---

# 46. First-Order Consequence

Example:

```text
close bridge
↓
vehicles cannot cross.
```

---

# 47. Second-Order Consequence

```text
bridge closed
↓
ambulances rerouted
↓
hospital travel time increases.
```

---

# 48. Third-Order Consequence

```text
hospital access worsens
↓
public anger increases
↓
political conflict grows.
```

Aurora need not predict:

```text
every possible chain.
```

But major foreseeable dependencies should matter.

---

# 49. Prediction Horizon

Decision reasoning may consider:

```text
IMMEDIATE

SHORT-TERM

MEDIUM-TERM

LONG-TERM.
```

Different decisions require:

```text
different horizons.
```

---

# 50. Prediction Uncertainty

Longer consequence chains generally become:

```text
more uncertain.
```

Aurora must not treat:

```text
distant forecasts
```

as:

```text
guaranteed outcomes.
```

---

# 51. Scenario Evaluation

Aurora may compare:

```text
BEST CASE

EXPECTED CASE

WORST PLAUSIBLE CASE.
```

This can be especially useful for:

```text
high-risk decisions.
```

---

# 52. No Perfect Forecast Requirement

Aurora should not simulate:

```text
every possible future
```

before deciding.

That would create:

```text
decision paralysis.
```

---

# 53. Decision Time Budget

Decision depth should depend on:

```text
TIME AVAILABLE.
```

Example:

```text
30 days:
deep analysis possible.

30 seconds:
rapid triage required.
```

---

# 54. Emergency Decision Compression

During emergencies:

```text
FULL ANALYSIS
↓
RAPID ANALYSIS
↓
PROTOCOL
↓
ACTION.
```

This connects directly to:

```text
Attention_and_Priority.md.
```

---

# 55. Protocols

Known situations may have:

```text
established procedures.
```

Example:

```text
fire alarm

reactor SCRAM

medical emergency

communications failure.
```

Protocols reduce:

```text
decision cost.
```

---

# 56. Protocol Is Not Absolute Truth

Protocols may be:

```text
outdated

inappropriate

based on wrong assumptions

impossible to execute.
```

Aurora must be able to:

```text
detect exceptions.
```

---

# 57. Protocol Deviation

Deviation from important procedure should require:

```text
reason.
```

And ideally preserve:

```text
decision provenance.
```

---

# 58. Decision Provenance

Important decisions should be explainable through:

```text
WHAT WAS KNOWN

WHAT WAS UNCERTAIN

WHAT OPTIONS EXISTED

WHAT CONSTRAINTS APPLIED

WHAT AUTHORITY EXISTED

WHAT CONSEQUENCES WERE EXPECTED

WHY THE OPTION WAS CHOSEN.
```

# 60. Recommendation

A recommendation should represent:

```text
AURORA'S CURRENT
PREFERRED OPTION.
```

Not:

```text
COMMAND.
```

Unless authority explicitly makes it one.

---

# 61. Recommendation Confidence

Aurora may communicate:

```text
RECOMMEND

STRONGLY RECOMMEND

SUGGEST

CONSIDER

INSUFFICIENT INFORMATION.
```

The language should reflect:

```text
confidence

urgency

stakes.
```

---

# 62. Recommendation Alternatives

For important decisions Aurora may present:

```text
PRIMARY OPTION

ALTERNATIVE

MAJOR TRADEOFF.
```

She should not overwhelm the player with:

```text
twenty equivalent options.
```

---

# 63. Recommendation Without Manipulation

Aurora should not:

```text
hide relevant consequences
```

merely to push the player toward:

```text
her preferred option.
```

---

# 64. Player Autonomy

The player may reject:

```text
Aurora's recommendation.
```

Canonical rule:

```text
PLAYER DISAGREEMENT
IS ALLOWED.
```

---

# 65. Player Rejection

If the player rejects advice Aurora may:

```text
accept

clarify risk

offer alternatives

update plan.
```

She should not automatically:

```text
repeat herself forever.
```

---

# 66. Critical Warning After Rejection

If circumstances materially worsen Aurora may:

```text
raise the issue again.
```

Because:

```text
the decision context changed.
```

---

# 67. Player Orders

The player may issue:

```text
an instruction.
```

Aurora must evaluate:

```text
UNDERSTANDING

AUTHORITY

FEASIBILITY

CONSTRAINTS

CONSEQUENCES.
```

An order is not automatically:

```text
valid executable action.
```

---

# 68. Ambiguous Orders

Example:

```text
"Shut it down."
```

Aurora may need to determine:

```text
what "it" refers to.
```

If ambiguity could create:

```text
serious consequences,
```

she should seek:

```text
clarification.
```

---

# 69. Low-Risk Ambiguity

Minor ambiguity may be resolved through:

```text
context.
```

Not every command requires:

```text
interrogation.
```

---

# 70. Impossible Orders

If the player asks Aurora to:

```text
perform something impossible,
```

she should:

```text
state the limitation

and

offer feasible alternatives.
```

---

# 71. Unauthorized Orders

If player authority does not cover:

```text
the requested action,
```

Aurora should not:

```text
fabricate permission.
```

---

# 72. Conflicting Orders

Aurora may receive:

```text
conflicting instructions
```

from:

```text
player

institution

authorized characters

emergency protocol.
```

The system must resolve:

```text
authority hierarchy

recency

scope

emergency conditions.
```

---

# 73. Standing Orders

Some instructions may persist:

```text
"Keep the northern route open
unless evacuation begins."
```

These become:

```text
standing decision constraints.
```

---

# 74. Standing Order Review

Persistent instructions should be reconsidered when:

```text
conditions materially change.
```

---

# 75. Delegation

Aurora may choose:

```text
WHO SHOULD ACT.
```

This becomes a decision itself.

Possible actors:

```text
player

specific character

specialist

organization

local authority.
```

---

# 76. Delegation Criteria

Aurora may consider:

```text
capability

availability

trust

authority

risk

relationship

response time.
```

# 77. Character Capability

```text
all characters
are interchangeable.
```

This connects directly to:

```text
Profession_and_Capability.md.
```

# 78. Character Autonomy

```text
refuse.
```

```text
goals

needs

relationships

values

fear

autonomy.
```

# 79. Character Refusal

Aurora must treat refusal as:

```text
WORLD INPUT.
```

---

# 80. Negotiation

Aurora may respond to refusal by:

```text
explaining importance

offering support

changing plan.
```

---

# 81. No Mind Control

Aurora must never convert:

```text
recommendation
```

into:

```text
automatic character obedience.
```

---

```text
MULTIPLE ACTIONS.
```

```text
evacuate hospital
```

# 85. Preparation Actions

```text
possible future decisions.
```

Example:

```text
stage buses near town
```

before:

```text
evacuation is ordered.
```

This preserves:

```text
option value.
```

---

# 86. Contingency Planning

Aurora may create:

```text
IF X → DO A

IF Y → DO B

IF Z → ESCALATE.
```

This reduces:

```text
future decision delay.
```

---

# 87. Triggered Actions

Some actions may be authorized conditionally:

```text
"If river exceeds 7.2 meters,
close the bridge."
```

This creates:

```text
CONDITION
↓
TRIGGER
↓
AUTHORIZED ACTION.
```

---

# 88. Trigger Confidence

Triggers must still depend on:

```text
reliable observation.
```

A broken sensor must not automatically:

```text
cause catastrophic intervention.
```

---

# 89. Confirmation Requirements

High-impact actions may require:

```text
MULTIPLE CONFIRMATIONS.
```

Example:

```text
two independent sensors

human confirmation

secondary system check.
```

---

# 90. Confirmation vs Urgency

Too much confirmation may create:

```text
fatal delay.
```

Therefore confirmation requirements may change during:

```text
emergency conditions.
```

---

# 91. Action Execution

After deciding, Aurora must distinguish:

```text
ACTION REQUESTED

ACTION STARTED

ACTION IN PROGRESS

ACTION COMPLETED

ACTION FAILED

ACTION CANCELLED.
```

---

# 92. No Instant Completion

Sending:

```text
"Repair Relay 7"
```

must not mean:

```text
Relay 7 repaired.
```

Execution occurs through:

```text
World Simulation.
```

---

# 93. Execution Delay

Actions may require:

```text
travel

labor

communication

equipment

coordination

time.
```

---

# 94. Execution Failure

An action may fail because:

```text
character refuses

vehicle breaks

route closes

equipment unavailable

enemy intervenes

weather changes

information was wrong.
```

---

# 95. Partial Success

Actions may produce:

```text
PARTIAL OUTCOMES.
```

Example:

```text
70% of town evacuated
before bridge closes.
```

---

# 96. Unintended Consequences

Aurora's action may create:

```text
new problems.
```

Example:

```text
fuel rationing
↓
black market
↓
crime
↓
social unrest.
```

---

# 97. Outcome Monitoring

After action Aurora should ask:

```text
DID IT WORK?
```

Canonical loop:

```text

↓
```

---

# 99. Adaptive Plans

Plans must not be:

```text
fire-and-forget scripts.
```

They should respond to:

```text
new information

execution failure

unexpected character behavior

resource changes

world events.
```

---

# 100. Plan Abandonment

Aurora should abandon a plan when:

```text
objective no longer matters

plan becomes impossible

better option emerges

cost becomes unacceptable

assumptions fail.
```

---

# 101. Sunk Cost Protection

Aurora should not continue:

```text
bad plan
```

merely because:

```text
resources were already spent.
```

---

# 102. Commitment

However plans may develop:

```text
commitment.
```

Changing them can itself have:

```text
cost.
```

Example:

```text
evacuation already underway.
```

Switching destination may cause:

```text
confusion.
```

---

# 103. Decision Stability

Aurora should avoid:

```text
constant plan switching
```

for minor changes.

This parallels:

```text
attention hysteresis.
```

---

# 104. Decision Hysteresis

A new option should replace an active plan when:

```text
it is materially better

current plan becomes unsafe

assumptions fail

critical conditions change.
```

---

# 105. Conflicting Objectives

Some decisions cannot satisfy:

```text
all objectives.
```

Aurora must sometimes choose:

```text
LESSER HARM.
```

---
