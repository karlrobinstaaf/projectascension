> *"A believable character does not always make the best decision. They make the decision that makes sense to them at that moment."*

---

# Purpose

The Decision Making system defines how characters in Project Ascension choose whether to act, what to do, when to do it, and what they are willing to sacrifice.

It connects:

```text
Needs

Motivations

Goals

Plans

Knowledge

Beliefs

Relationships

Values

Capabilities

Resources

Risk

Time

Emotion

World Conditions
```

into:

```text
DECISIONS.
```

The purpose is not to create:

```text
PERFECT OPTIMIZERS.
```

The purpose is to create:

```text
BELIEVABLE PEOPLE
MAKING PLAUSIBLE CHOICES
FROM LIMITED INFORMATION.
```

---

# Core Principle

Characters do not ask:

```text
WHAT IS OBJECTIVELY
THE BEST ACTION?
```

They effectively ask:

```text
GIVEN WHAT I KNOW,
WHAT I BELIEVE,
WHAT I WANT,
WHAT I FEAR,
WHO I CARE ABOUT,
WHAT I CAN DO,
AND HOW MUCH TIME I HAVE—

WHAT SHOULD I DO?
```

---

# Decision Foundation

Every meaningful decision emerges from:

```text
CHARACTER STATE
+
NEEDS
+
MOTIVATIONS
+
GOALS
+
PLANS
+
KNOWLEDGE
+
BELIEFS
+
VALUES
+
RELATIONSHIPS
+
CAPABILITIES
+
RESOURCES
+
WORLD CONDITIONS
+
TIME PRESSURE.
```

Conceptually:

```text
CURRENT STATE
      ↓
DECISION TRIGGER
      ↓
PERCEIVED SITUATION
      ↓
RELEVANT GOALS
      ↓
POSSIBLE ACTIONS
      ↓
EXPECTED OUTCOMES
      ↓
RISK / COST / VALUE
      ↓
CHOICE
      ↓
ACTION
      ↓
CONSEQUENCE
      ↓
MEMORY / LEARNING.
```

---

# Perceived Reality

Decision Making operates upon:

```text
CHARACTER PERCEIVED REALITY.
```

Not:

```text
WORLD TRUTH.
```

Therefore:

```text
World Truth:
Road is safe.

Character Belief:
Road is dangerous.

Decision:
Take longer route.
```

This is:

```text
VALID.
```

The decision may be objectively unnecessary while remaining completely rational from the character's perspective.

---

# Decision Trigger

Characters should not continuously reconsider every possible action.

Decisions occur when something creates:

```text
A REASON TO CHOOSE.
```

Recommended trigger categories:

```text
NEED CHANGE

GOAL CHANGE

PLAN FAILURE

NEW INFORMATION

WORLD EVENT

RELATIONSHIP EVENT

OPPORTUNITY

THREAT

RESOURCE CHANGE

TIME THRESHOLD

ROLE OBLIGATION

DIRECT REQUEST

CONFLICT

INTERRUPTION

FAILURE

SUCCESS

EMOTIONAL EVENT.
```

---

# Need Trigger

Example:

```text
Food Need:
LOW
↓
No Action
```

Later:

```text
Food Need:
CRITICAL
↓
Decision Required
```

Possible choices:

```text
buy food

request help

hunt

trade

steal

travel

wait.
```

---

# Goal Trigger

A newly created Goal may require:

```text
planning

resource allocation

information seeking

travel

communication

commitment.
```

Example:

```text
Goal:
Repair generator.
```

Decision:

```text
Repair personally?

Ask mechanic?

Buy replacement?

Delay repair?

Abandon generator?
```

---

# Plan Failure Trigger

Example:

```text
Goal:
Reach Millhaven.

Plan:
Use eastern bridge.

New Knowledge:
Bridge destroyed.
```

This produces:

```text
PLAN FAILURE
      ↓
REASSESSMENT
      ↓
NEW DECISION.
```

---

# Information Trigger

New information may cause:

```text
no change

minor adjustment

plan change

goal change

urgent action.
```

The importance depends on:

```text
relevance

confidence

risk

time

goal impact.
```

---

# Opportunity Trigger

Example:

```text
Character learns
an unused workshop
is available cheaply.
```

Possible result:

```text
NEW OPPORTUNITY
      ↓
COMPARE WITH CURRENT GOALS
      ↓
PURSUE / IGNORE / DELAY.
```

---

# Threat Trigger

Example:

```text
Wildfire approaching settlement.
```

Possible choices:

```text
evacuate

protect property

help others

wait for confirmation

seek family

assist authorities.
```

Different characters may choose differently.

# Decision Scope

Decisions exist at multiple scales.

Recommended conceptual levels:

```text
REFLEXIVE

ROUTINE

TACTICAL

STRATEGIC

LIFE-CHANGING.
```

---

# Reflexive Decisions

Examples:

```text
take cover

grab falling object

run from immediate danger

help nearby injured person.
```

These require:

```text
minimal deliberation.
```

---

# Routine Decisions

Examples:

```text
go to work

buy groceries

take usual route

call friend

perform maintenance.
```

These may often use:

```text
HABIT.
```

---

# Tactical Decisions

Examples:

```text
which route to take

whether to spend limited fuel

whether to trust a report

whether to help someone

whether to repair or replace equipment.
```

---

# Strategic Decisions

Examples:

```text
expand business

join faction

relocate

change profession

support political proposal

prepare for winter.
```

---

# Life-Changing Decisions

Examples:

```text
marriage

divorce

migration

career change

betrayal

major sacrifice

leadership commitment

having children

permanent alliance.
```

These decisions should usually require:

```text
HIGH MOTIVATIONAL WEIGHT

and/or

SIGNIFICANT CIRCUMSTANCES.
```

---

# Decision Resolution

Decision complexity should determine:

```text
SIMULATION COST.
```

Routine decisions may use:

```text
simple heuristics.
```

Major decisions may require:

```text
full evaluation.
```

---

# Candidate Actions

A decision requires:

```text
POSSIBLE ACTIONS.
```

But characters do not consider:

```text
EVERY THEORETICALLY POSSIBLE ACTION.
```

They generate a limited:

```text
ACTION SET.
```

---

# Action Generation

Possible actions may derive from:

```text
current Plans

past experience

profession

skills

resources

relationships

known opportunities

social norms

habits

environment.
```

---

# Knowledge Limits Options

A character cannot choose an option they:

```text
DO NOT KNOW EXISTS.
```

Example:

```text
Hidden mountain road exists.
```

Character does not know about it.

Therefore:

```text
Hidden road
NOT IN ACTION SET.
```

---

# Capability Limits Options

A character may know an option exists but believe:

```text
I CANNOT DO THAT.
```

Example:

```text
Repair generator personally.
```

If perceived capability is low:

```text
option may be rejected.
```

---

# Perceived Capability

Decision Making should use:

```text
PERCEIVED CAPABILITY
```

during choice evaluation.

Actual capability matters during:

```text
ACTION RESOLUTION.
```

Therefore:

```text
Actual Skill:
HIGH

Self-Belief:
LOW

Decision:
Hire someone else.
```

is valid.

---

# Impossible Actions

Actions that are genuinely impossible should normally be excluded.

Examples:

```text
travel without route

purchase unavailable item

call someone without communication

use equipment not possessed.
```

But characters may sometimes:

```text
ATTEMPT THE IMPOSSIBLE
```

if they incorrectly believe it is possible.

---

# Option Filtering

Candidate actions may be filtered by:

```text
knowledge

perceived capability

resources

time

location

social constraints

values

risk tolerance

legal constraints

relationship obligations.
```

---

# Action Evaluation

For each plausible option, characters may evaluate:

```text
Goal Progress

Need Satisfaction

Expected Benefit

Expected Cost

Risk

Time

Resource Cost

Relationship Impact

Value Alignment

Role Alignment

Future Opportunity

Reversibility

Confidence.
```

---

# Conceptual Utility

Decision Making may conceptually estimate:

```text
PERCEIVED VALUE
=
EXPECTED BENEFIT
-
EXPECTED COST
-
PERCEIVED RISK
+
GOAL ALIGNMENT
+
VALUE ALIGNMENT
+
RELATIONSHIP IMPACT
+
ROLE ALIGNMENT
+
EMOTIONAL WEIGHT.
```

This is:

```text
A CONCEPTUAL MODEL.
```

It should not require every decision to use:

```text
one universal numerical formula.
```

---

# Bounded Rationality

Characters have:

```text
LIMITED INFORMATION

LIMITED TIME

LIMITED ATTENTION

LIMITED COMPUTATION

LIMITED EXPERIENCE.
```

Therefore:

```text
PERFECT OPTIMIZATION
IS NOT EXPECTED.
```

---

# Satisficing

Characters may choose:

```text
THE FIRST OPTION
THAT IS GOOD ENOUGH.
```

rather than:

```text
THE BEST POSSIBLE OPTION.
```

This is especially likely when:

```text
decision is routine

stakes are low

time is limited

character is tired

many choices exist.
```

---

# Good Enough Threshold

Conceptually:

```text
OPTION VALUE
≥
ACCEPTABLE THRESHOLD
      ↓
CHOOSE.
```

The threshold may vary by:

```text
personality

stakes

urgency

experience

risk.
```

---

# Decision Heuristics

Characters may use simple rules.

Examples:

```text
"Use the route I know."

"Ask Mara about medical issues."

"Never borrow money."

"Call family first."

"Trust official weather alerts."

"Buy when prices fall below X."
```

Heuristics reduce:

```text
DECISION COST.
```

---

# Habit

Repeated decisions may become:

```text
HABITUAL.
```

Conceptually:

```text
REPEATED SUCCESSFUL ACTION
      ↓
REDUCED DELIBERATION
      ↓
HABIT.
```

---

# Habit Advantage

Habits allow characters to:

```text
act quickly

reduce cognitive load

maintain routines.
```

---

# Habit Risk

Habits may become inappropriate when:

```text
WORLD CONDITIONS CHANGE.
```

Example:

```text
Character always uses eastern road.

Bridge destroyed.

Character has not heard.
```

Habit may produce:

```text
failed travel attempt.
```

---

# Risk

Risk represents:

```text
UNCERTAIN NEGATIVE OUTCOME.
```

Characters evaluate risk based on:

```text
perceived probability

perceived severity.
```

Conceptually:

```text
PERCEIVED RISK
=
PERCEIVED PROBABILITY
×
PERCEIVED CONSEQUENCE.
```

---

# Risk Is Subjective

Two characters may assess:

```text
THE SAME SITUATION
```

differently.

Example:

```text
Mountain road during storm.
```

Experienced driver:

```text
Risk:
MODERATE.
```

Inexperienced driver:

```text
Risk:
HIGH.
```

---

# Risk Tolerance

Characters differ in willingness to accept risk.

Possible tendencies:

```text
RISK AVERSE

CAUTIOUS

BALANCED

RISK TOLERANT

RECKLESS.
```

These should be tendencies rather than absolute behavior rules.

---

# Domain-Specific Risk

A character may be:

```text
financially cautious
```

but:

```text
physically brave.
```

Risk tolerance should therefore support:

```text
DOMAIN DEPENDENCE.
```

---

# High Consequence Risk

Even low probability may matter when consequences are severe.

Example:

```text
Bridge Collapse Probability:
LOW

Consequence:
DEATH.
```

A cautious character may choose:

```text
DO NOT CROSS.
```

---

# Risk Under Uncertainty

Characters may not know:

```text
probability.
```

They may reason from:

```text
experience

warnings

intuition

trust

fear

professional expertise.
```

---

# Unknown Risk

Some characters respond to uncertainty with:

```text
CAUTION.
```

Others respond with:

```text
ASSUMPTION OF SAFETY.
```

Others:

```text
SEEK INFORMATION.
```

# Goal Priority

# Dynamic Priority

Example:


During fire:


# Goal Conflict

Two Goals may directly conflict.

Example:

```text
Goal:
Protect friend.

Goal:
Tell truth to authorities.
```

```text
MORAL / RELATIONSHIP CONFLICT.
```

---

# Tradeoffs

Meaningful decisions often involve:

```text
GAIN SOMETHING

LOSE SOMETHING.
```

The engine should support choices where:

```text
NO OPTION IS PERFECT.
```

---

# Value Conflict

```text
internal conflict.
```

Example:

```text
Value:
Honesty

```

```text
lie.
```

Decision requires:

```text
VALUE PRIORITIZATION.
```

---

# Value Alignment

Actions consistent with strong Values receive:

```text
positive decision weight.
```

Actions violating strong Values receive:

```text
negative decision weight.
```

But:

```text
VALUES ARE NOT HARD LOCKS.
```

---

# Value Violation

Characters may violate Values under:

```text
fear

desperation

love

pressure

survival

conflicting values.
```

Such decisions may produce:

```text
guilt

regret

self-belief change

relationship consequences.
```

# Relationship Influence

```text
care

trust

loyalty

fear

obligation

love

dependency

history.
```

---

# Relationship Priority

A character may sacrifice:

```text
money

time

safety

career

reputation
```

for someone important.

This should emerge from:

```text
RELATIONSHIP STATE
+
VALUES
```

# Relationship Requests

When another character asks for help, decision evaluation may include:

```text
relationship strength

trust

request cost

request urgency

current Goals.
```

---

# Reciprocity

Characters may remember:

```text
past help

past refusal

past sacrifice.
```

Example:

```text
"You helped me when I needed it."
```

This may increase willingness to:

```text
accept cost now.
```

---

# Resentment

Past relationship damage may reduce willingness to:

```text
help

trust

cooperate.
```

Even when cooperation would be objectively useful.

---

# Role Obligation

```text

doctor

leader

employee

partner

community member.
```

Roles create:

```text
EXPECTED BEHAVIOR.
```

# Role Conflict

```text

HIGH-WEIGHT DECISION.
```

```text
goal priority.
```

```text
RANDOMNESS.
```

# Emotional Intensity

# Emotion Does Not Remove Agency

```text
characters remain characters.
```

```text
confidence

heuristic
```


---

# Attribution

Characters interpret:

```text
WHY AN OUTCOME OCCURRED.
```

This affects learning.

---

# Correct Attribution

```text
Plan failed
because fuel ran out.
```

Character learns:

```text
carry reserve fuel.
```

---

# Incorrect Attribution

```text
Plan failed
because fuel ran out.
```

Character believes:

```text
Mara gave bad directions.
```

Future relationship:

```text
trust decreases.
```

---

# Success Bias

A risky decision that succeeds may:

```text
increase future risk tolerance.
```

Even when:

```text
decision was objectively poor.
```

---

# Failure Bias

A good decision that fails due to bad luck may:

```text
reduce confidence.
```

---

# Luck

Decision quality and outcome quality must remain separate.

Critical distinction:

```text
GOOD DECISION
+
BAD OUTCOME
```
