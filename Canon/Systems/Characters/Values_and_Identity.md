---

# 1. Purpose

The Values and Identity system defines the Character-level structures that answer:

```text
WHAT MATTERS
TO THIS PERSON?

WHAT DO THEY
CONSIDER WORTH
PROTECTING?

WHAT DO THEY
CONSIDER WORTH
SACRIFICING FOR?

WHAT DO THEY
REFUSE TO
BECOME?

WHO DO THEY
BELIEVE THEY ARE?

WHO DO THEY
WANT TO REMAIN?

WHAT ACTIONS
WOULD CREATE
INTERNAL CONFLICT?

WHAT ACTIONS
WOULD THREATEN
THEIR IDENTITY?
```

The system exists to provide:

```text
MORAL

PERSONAL

IDENTITY-BASED

AND VALUE-BASED
PRESSURE
```

without scripting behavior.

---

# 2. Core Principle

Values and Identity influence:

```text
WHAT FEELS
IMPORTANT

WHAT FEELS
ACCEPTABLE

WHAT FEELS
WRONG

WHAT FEELS
WORTH THE COST

WHAT FEELS
LIKE BETRAYAL

WHAT FEELS
LIKE SELF-BETRAYAL.
```

They do not determine:

```text
WHAT THE CHARACTER
MUST DO.
```

Therefore:

```text
VALUE
≠
COMMAND

IDENTITY
≠
SCRIPT.
```

# 6. Value Definition

A Value is:

> **Something a Character considers important, worth protecting, worth pursuing, worth preserving, or worth accepting meaningful cost for.**

Examples may include:

```text
FAMILY

LOYALTY

FREEDOM

DUTY

SECURITY

HONESTY

JUSTICE

COMPASSION

COMMUNITY

TRADITION

ACHIEVEMENT

KNOWLEDGE

STATUS

INDEPENDENCE

RESPONSIBILITY

EQUALITY

ADVENTURE

CREATIVITY

PEACE

POWER

FAITH

ORDER.
```

These are not universal categories.

They are conceptual examples.

---

# 7. Values Are Character-Specific

Two Characters may both value:

```text
FAMILY
```

but mean very different things.

Character A:

```text
FAMILY
=
PROTECT CHILDREN
AT ANY COST.
```

Character B:

```text
FAMILY
=
PRESERVE FAMILY
NAME AND LEGACY.
```

Character C:

```text
FAMILY
=
NEVER ABANDON
RELATIVES.
```

The label alone is insufficient.

Meaning matters.

---

Some Values require a target.

Example:

```text
LOYALTY
```

is incomplete without:

```text
LOYAL TO WHOM?
```

Possible targets:

```text

FAMILY

COMMUNITY

INSTITUTION

FACTION

COUNTRY

PROFESSION

PROMISE

CAUSE.
```

# 9. Value Context

A Value may apply differently across contexts.

Example:

```text
HONESTY
```

may be strongly valued in:

```text
PERSONAL RELATIONSHIPS
```

while the Character accepts:

```text
STRATEGIC DECEPTION
```

in:

```text
MILITARY

SECURITY

POLITICAL

PROFESSIONAL
```

contexts.

This is not automatically hypocrisy.

Context matters.

---

# 10. Value Strength

Not every Value matters equally.

Conceptual strength descriptors may include:

```text
CORE

STRONG

MODERATE

WEAK

LATENT

IRRELEVANT.
```

These are conceptual.

They are not mandatory numerical tiers.

# 11. Core Value

A Core Value is:

> **A Value deeply integrated into the Character's identity, long-term priorities or moral self-understanding.**

Core Values may strongly influence:

```text
MAJOR GOALS

SACRIFICE

MORAL BOUNDARIES

RELATIONSHIPS

LONG-TERM CHOICES

SELF-CONCEPT.
```

But:

```text
CORE VALUE
DOES NOT
GUARANTEE
BEHAVIOR.
```

---

# 12. Value Priority

Characters may hold several important Values.

The system may represent:

```text
RELATIVE PRIORITY
```

without requiring:

```text
A PERMANENT
RANKED LIST.
```

Example:

```text
FAMILY

DUTY

HONESTY

SECURITY

FREEDOM.
```

Their relative influence may change with context.

---

# 13. No Permanent Value Ladder

Avoid:

```text
1. FAMILY
2. DUTY
3. HONESTY
```

as an absolute behavioral law.

A Character may normally prioritize Family above Duty.

But one extreme professional situation may reverse that.

Values create:

```text
PRESSURE.
```

They do not create:

```text
AUTOMATIC OUTPUT.
```

---

# 14. Value Conflict

A Value conflict exists when:

```text
TWO OR MORE
IMPORTANT VALUES

SUPPORT
INCOMPATIBLE
DIRECTIONS.
```

Example:

```text
HONESTY

VERSUS

LOYALTY.
```

A friend commits wrongdoing.

The Character may face:

```text
TELL THE TRUTH

OR

PROTECT FRIEND.
```

Both options may violate something important.

---

# 15. Value Conflict Is Desirable

Do not eliminate Value conflict.

It creates:

```text
TRADEOFF

REGRET

SACRIFICE

HESITATION

INTERNAL PRESSURE

IDENTITY QUESTIONING

RELATIONSHIP CONSEQUENCE.
```

Decision Making resolves the current choice.

Values and Identity preserves the conflict.

---



# 18. No Moral Alignment System

# 20. Duty vs Desire

A Character may:

```text
NOT WANT
TO DO SOMETHING

BUT

STILL BELIEVE

```

```text

Leave hospital

```

# 21. Loyalty

Loyalty represents:

> **A Character's value-based commitment toward a particular Actor, group, institution, cause, identity or relationship.**

Always ask:

```text
LOYAL
TO WHOM

OR

TO WHAT?
```

---

# 22. Loyalty Is Not Relationship Trust

A Character may be:

```text
LOYAL
TO SOMEONE
```

while:

```text
NOT FULLY
TRUSTING THEM.
```

Likewise they may trust:

```text
AN EXPERT
```

without feeling:

```text
LOYALTY.
```

Relationship Trust remains authoritative elsewhere.

---


# 39. Identity Definition

Identity represents:

> **The Character's current self-understanding of who they are, what kind of person they believe themselves to be, and which roles or principles they consider part of themselves.**

Examples:

```text
"I AM A DOCTOR."

"I AM A FATHER."

"I AM SOMEONE
WHO KEEPS PROMISES."

"I PROTECT PEOPLE."

"I AM INDEPENDENT."

"I AM A SURVIVOR."

"I DO NOT ABANDON
MY FAMILY."
```

---

# 40. Identity vs Self-Belief

Important distinction:

```text
SELF-BELIEF

"I AM BAD
AT MECHANICAL REPAIR."
```

belongs primarily to:

```text
Knowledge and Beliefs.
```

But:

```text
IDENTITY

"I AM A PERSON
WHO SOLVES PROBLEMS."
```

belongs here.

One concerns:

```text
WHAT I THINK
I CAN DO.
```

The other concerns:

```text
WHO I BELIEVE
I AM.
```

---

# 41. Identity vs Role

A Character may hold a role without internalizing it.

Example:

```text
ROLE:
Community Leader

IDENTITY:
"I AM JUST
TEMPORARILY HELPING."
```

Another Character may deeply internalize:

```text
"I AM RESPONSIBLE
FOR THIS COMMUNITY."
```

Same role.

Different identity.

---

# 42. Professional Identity

Profession may become part of Identity.

Example:

```text
PROFESSION:
Physician

"I SAVE LIVES."
```

```text
WHAT THAT ROLE
MEANS TO THE PERSON.
```

# 47. Identity Conflict

Identity conflict occurs when:

```text
MULTIPLE
SELF-UNDERSTANDINGS

OR

IDENTITY AND ACTION

BECOME
INCOMPATIBLE.
```

Example:

```text
"I AM A GOOD FATHER."

VERSUS

"I MUST LEAVE
MY FAMILY
TO PERFORM MY DUTY."
```

---

# 48. Identity and Action Conflict

Example:

```text
IDENTITY:
"I AM HONEST."

ACTION:
Character lies.
```

This may create:

```text
INTERNAL PRESSURE.
```

Values and Identity records:

```text
THE CONFLICT.
```

Psychology determines current emotional response.

Memory preserves the experience.

Development determines possible long-term change.

---

# 49. Identity Pressure

```text
BEHAVIOR CONSISTENT
WITH SELF-CONCEPT.
```


# 50. Identity Protection

```text

DENYING

```

> **An action or category of action that a Character considers deeply unacceptable, identity-threatening, or morally prohibited.**

Examples may include:

```text

KILL INNOCENT PERSON

BETRAY FAMILY

ABANDON PATIENT

TORTURE

STEAL FROM FRIEND

ACCEPT BRIBE

BREAK SACRED PROMISE.
```

---

# 53. Moral Boundary Strength

Conceptually:

```text

```

```text
RARE.
```

# 61. Preference Definition

A Preference is:

> **Something a Character likes, favors or finds comfortable without necessarily considering it morally important.**

Examples:

```text
QUIET PLACES

CITY LIFE

RURAL LIFE

MORNING ROUTINE

WORKING ALONE

SPECIFIC FOOD

MUSIC

FORMALITY

TRAVEL.
```

---

# 62. Preference vs Value

Example:

```text
PREFERENCE:
Rural life

VALUE:
Family.
```

Character may move to a city because:

```text
FAMILY
MATTERS MORE.
```

Values often create more pressure than simple Preferences.

But this is not an absolute law.

---

# 63. Aversion Definition

An Aversion is:

> **Something a Character strongly dislikes, avoids or finds internally costly without necessarily considering it morally wrong.**

Examples:

```text
CROWDS

CONFLICT

PUBLIC ATTENTION

DEBT

UNCERTAINTY

DEPENDENCE

BUREAUCRACY

HEIGHTS

ISOLATION.
```

---

# 64. Aversion Is Not Moral Boundary

Example:

```text
AVERSION:
Crowds

MORAL BOUNDARY:
Do not abandon child.
```

These operate at completely different levels.

---

# 65. Aversion Is Not Prohibition

Characters may act against aversion when:

```text
GOAL

VALUE

RELATIONSHIP

EMERGENCY

RESPONSIBILITY
```

creates sufficient pressure.

---

# 66. Behavioral Friction

Values, Identity, Preferences and Aversions may create:

```text
INTERNAL FRICTION.
```

Example:

```text
IDENTITY:
Highly self-reliant

ACTION OPTION:
Ask for help.
```

The action remains possible.

It may simply carry:

```text
HIGHER
INTERNAL COST.
```

---

# 67. Behavioral Friction Is Not Capability

A Character may be:

```text
FULLY CAPABLE
```

of an action while finding it:

```text
DEEPLY UNCOMFORTABLE.
```

Capability and internal friction are separate.

---

# 168. Closing Principle

Human beings are not perfectly consistent moral systems.

They hold competing Values.

They make promises they fail to keep.

They believe themselves to be one kind of person and sometimes discover evidence that they are another.

They sacrifice things they thought were essential.

They protect things they did not realize mattered.

They rationalize.

They regret.

They change.

But change should have history.

Project Ascension therefore treats Values and Identity not as:

```text
BEHAVIORAL COMMANDS
```

but as:

```text
THE INTERNAL
STRUCTURES THAT
MAKE DECISIONS
PERSONALLY MEANINGFUL.
```

The central principle is:

> **Values define what matters. Identity defines who the Character believes they are. Neither determines behavior — but both determine what a choice may cost them internally.**
