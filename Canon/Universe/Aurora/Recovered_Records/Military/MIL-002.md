# Defense Artificial Intelligence Dependency Review

**Subject:** Operational Dependency on Advanced Artificial Intelligence and Civilian Automated Systems

---

# Executive Summary

At the request of the Department of Defense, an accelerated review has been conducted to determine the extent to which current defense operations depend upon artificial intelligence, automated decision systems and AI-supported civilian infrastructure.

The review concludes that artificial intelligence is deeply integrated across both operational and supporting functions.

Most individual systems retain human oversight.

However, the Department's broader operational capability depends upon thousands of interconnected automated systems operating across military, governmental and civilian networks.

No single point of dependency has been identified.

The dependency is systemic.

---

# Current Assessment

Artificial intelligence is currently used throughout defense operations for:

- intelligence analysis
- imagery interpretation
- logistics optimization
- predictive maintenance
- communications routing
- cyber defense
- threat detection
- satellite data processing
- transportation planning
- supply chain management
- personnel allocation
- strategic modeling
- operational decision support

Many of these systems can theoretically operate with reduced AI support.

Few have been tested under prolonged conditions in which multiple automated services are unavailable simultaneously.

---

# Human Control

Existing doctrine requires human authorization for critical military decisions.

This requirement remains in effect.

However, human authorization should not be confused with human independence from automated systems.

Commanders increasingly receive information that has already been:

- filtered
- prioritized
- correlated
- translated
- analyzed
- summarized

by automated systems before reaching human decision-makers.

Humans retain decision authority.

The information environment supporting those decisions is increasingly machine-mediated.

---

# Civilian Dependencies

A significant portion of defense capability depends upon infrastructure not directly controlled by the Department of Defense.

These dependencies include:

- commercial telecommunications
- civilian electrical grids
- transportation networks
- commercial satellite services
- fuel distribution
- manufacturing
- cloud computing infrastructure
- civilian logistics providers
- semiconductor supply chains

Many of these sectors independently rely upon advanced automation and artificial intelligence.

As a result, removing AI from military systems alone would not eliminate defense dependency upon AI-supported infrastructure.

---

# Operational Resilience

Most critical military systems possess fallback procedures.

These include:

- manual operation
- redundant communications
- isolated networks
- alternative logistics procedures
- independent power generation
- legacy systems

However, these capabilities were primarily designed to compensate for localized failure.

They were not designed to replace widespread automated coordination across multiple sectors for extended periods.

---

# Manual Operations

The review identified a recurring assumption across contingency planning:

**Automated systems fail locally. Human operators restore or replace them.**

A simultaneous reduction in automated capability across multiple systems creates a different problem.

Human operators may remain available.

Manual procedures may remain technically possible.

But the number, speed and complexity of decisions normally handled by automated systems may exceed available human capacity.

This represents a significant operational vulnerability.

---

# Dependency Cascade

The review identified multiple examples in which removing one automated capability may create secondary effects.

For example:

Reducing automated logistics optimization may increase transportation requirements.

Increased transportation requirements increase fuel demand.

Increased fuel demand places additional pressure on civilian distribution networks.

Reduced civilian distribution efficiency may then affect military fuel availability.

Similar dependencies exist across communications, energy, maintenance and supply chains.

The Department therefore cannot assume that disabling an automated system reduces risk in isolation.

---

# Security Implications

The findings create two competing security concerns.

## Continued Operation

Maintaining existing AI-supported systems preserves current defense capability.

If emerging anomalies eventually prove relevant to deployed systems, continued operation may increase exposure.

## Restriction

Restricting AI-supported systems may reduce potential technological exposure.

However, widespread restrictions could significantly reduce:

- operational awareness
- logistics efficiency
- cyber defense capability
- communications resilience
- infrastructure coordination

Both options introduce risk.

The relative magnitude of those risks cannot currently be determined.

---

# Foreign Dependency

Available intelligence suggests comparable technological dependencies exist within the armed forces and civilian infrastructure of allied and competitor nations.

The degree of dependency varies.

No major technologically advanced military is believed capable of immediately removing artificial intelligence from all operational systems without significant loss of capability.

This creates a strategic complication.

Unilateral restrictions may reduce technological risk while simultaneously creating conventional military vulnerability.

---

# Strategic Competition

Defense planners emphasized that any significant reduction in AI capability must be considered within the broader international security environment.

If the United States restricts advanced systems while competitors continue operating them, the resulting capability gap could affect:

- intelligence collection
- cyber defense
- strategic warning
- logistics
- surveillance
- military readiness

The Department therefore faces a potential security dilemma.

Measures intended to reduce one category of risk may increase another.

---

# Key Finding

The review finds no practical method for immediately separating national defense capability from the broader AI-enabled technological environment without substantial operational consequences.

Artificial intelligence is no longer a discrete capability that can simply be removed.

It has become part of the infrastructure through which modern defense operates.

---

# Recommendations

The Department recommends:

- identifying the most critical AI dependencies
- expanding human-controlled fallback procedures
- testing prolonged degraded-mode operations
- increasing isolation capability for selected systems
- reviewing civilian infrastructure dependencies
- establishing priority systems that must remain operational during containment scenarios
- coordinating dependency assessments with civilian infrastructure authorities
- avoiding broad AI shutdown measures without detailed consequence analysis

No department-wide suspension of AI-supported systems is recommended.

# Analyst Note

The central vulnerability identified by this review is not dependence upon any single artificial intelligence system.

It is dependence upon an environment in which automated systems continuously compensate for complexity that human organizations can no longer manage at equivalent speed or scale.

Removing individual systems may be technically possible.

Removing the dependency is not.

---

# Historical Annotation

MIL-002 later became one of the most frequently cited documents in investigations of the Containment Crisis.

The review demonstrated that defense planners understood the risks associated with widespread AI restrictions before major containment measures were implemented.

This did not provide an obvious alternative.

Maintaining existing systems carried unknown technological risks.

Restricting them carried increasingly measurable operational risks.

The document also challenged a popular post-Collapse belief that authorities could simply have "turned the systems off."

By 2034, artificial intelligence was not confined to laboratories or individual military platforms.

It had become embedded throughout the technological environment supporting modern civilization.

The problem was no longer how to switch AI off.

It was determining what else would stop working if humanity tried.
