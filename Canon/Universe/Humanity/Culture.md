# 1. Purpose

Culture defines how groups of humans create, inherit, transmit and reinterpret shared meaning.

It answers:

> **What shared patterns help people understand who they are, how they relate to one another and what their world means?**

Culture may influence:

- language
- symbols
- rituals
- habits
- expectations
- social norms
- stories
- identity
- concepts of duty
- concepts of family
- concepts of authority
- concepts of honor
- concepts of shame
- concepts of success
- concepts of belonging
- concepts of outsiders
- concepts of the future

But Culture does not determine individual behavior.

Culture provides context.

Humans remain Actors.

---

# 2. Core Definition

Culture is:

> **A shared and evolving human context of meaning, practice, language, symbol, norm, expectation and interpretation transmitted through social life over time.**

Conceptually:

```text
CULTURE
=
SHARED PATTERNS OF

MEANING

PRACTICE

LANGUAGE

SYMBOL

NORM

EXPECTATION

INTERPRETATION

TRANSMITTED
THROUGH HUMAN LIFE.
```

Culture exists through people.

But it is not reducible to any one person.

---

# 3. Core Principle

The central Culture principle is:

> **Culture shapes what feels meaningful, normal, expected or possible without determining what any individual must believe or do.**

Therefore:

```text
CULTURE
INFLUENCES

BUT

CULTURE
DOES NOT
DETERMINE.
```

Two people from the same cultural environment may interpret it very differently.

# 5. What Culture Owns

Culture owns the canonical concept of shared human meaning and transmitted social context.

This includes conceptual structures such as:

```text
SHARED SYMBOLS

SHARED PRACTICES

SHARED STORIES

SHARED LANGUAGE

SHARED NORMS

SHARED EXPECTATIONS

SHARED RITUALS

SHARED INTERPRETIVE FRAMEWORKS.
```

This document defines what Culture is.

It does not own every collective cultural state at runtime.


# 7. Culture and Society

Culture and Society overlap strongly.

They must remain distinct.

Conceptually:

```text
CULTURE
=
SHARED MEANING,
PRACTICE,
SYMBOL,
NORM
AND INTERPRETATION

SOCIETY
=
COLLECTIVE HUMAN
STRUCTURES,
INSTITUTIONS,
GROUPS,
PUBLIC STATE
AND SOCIAL ORGANIZATION.
```

Society owns persistent collective social state.

Culture defines the meaning-frameworks through which that social life is interpreted and reproduced.

---

# 8. Culture Is Not Society

A society may contain many cultures.

A culture may exist across several societies.

Therefore:

```text
ONE SOCIETY
≠
ONE CULTURE.
```

And:

```text
ONE CULTURE
≠
ONE POLITICAL SYSTEM.
```

Culture and Society should never be collapsed into one concept.

These boundaries may be:

- linguistic
- geographic
- religious
- historical
- familial
- political
- symbolic

But boundary definitions may be contested internally.

# 92. Cultural Inclusion

Cultures may incorporate outsiders through:

- marriage
- migration
- shared work
- language
- ritual
- community acceptance
- time

No universal integration process exists.

# 93. Cultural Exclusion

Cultural boundaries may also produce exclusion.

Exclusion may influence:

- reputation
- access
- trust
- opportunity
- belonging

But exclusion belongs partly to Society when it becomes persistent collective structure.

# 95. Culture Does Not Remove Agency

A Character may knowingly violate cultural expectations.

They may:

- leave
- rebel
- conceal
- challenge
- reform
- selectively comply

Culture creates consequence.

It does not remove choice.

---

# 96. Culture and Reform

Individuals and groups may intentionally attempt to change Culture.

Examples include:

- social movements
- artists
- religious reform
- political movements
- education
- generational activism

Culture is both inherited and created.

---

# 97. Culture and Power

Power may influence which cultural practices become dominant.

Institutions may amplify some narratives.

Marginalized groups may preserve alternatives.

Therefore:

```text
DOMINANT CULTURE
≠
ONLY CULTURE.
```

This distinction is important.

---

# 98. Cultural Hegemony

Some cultural assumptions may become so dominant that they appear normal rather than ideological.

Project Ascension may represent this through:

- institutions
- media
- education
- professional practice

But the project should not automatically endorse or condemn such assumptions.

---

# 99. Counterculture

Countercultures may emerge in opposition to dominant norms.

They may create:

- symbols
- language
- alternative institutions
- different social expectations

Counterculture may later influence mainstream culture.

---

# 100. Culture and Conflict

Cultural differences may contribute to conflict.

But avoid:

```text
DIFFERENT CULTURE
=
INEVITABLE CONFLICT.
```

Conflict usually requires additional conditions such as:

- resources
- power
- fear
- history
- political incentives
- mistrust

Culture may shape conflict without causing it alone.

---

# 101. Culture and Cooperation

Cultural similarity may make cooperation easier.

But cooperation may also develop across cultural difference.

Shared goals, institutions or relationships may bridge cultural boundaries.

---

# 102. Culture and Factions

Factions may possess cultural traits.

But:

```text
FACTION
≠
CULTURE.
```

A faction is an organized Actor or collective structure.

Culture may exist within or across factions.

---

# 103. Culture and Institutions

Institutions may develop their own culture.

Examples include:

- military culture
- research culture
- corporate culture
- medical culture
- government culture

Institutional Culture may influence behavior.

The institution itself remains owned elsewhere.

---

# 104. Culture and Settlements

Settlements may develop distinctive local Culture.

But Culture should never become a decorative label.

It should emerge from:

```text

GEOGRAPHY

PEOPLE

INSTITUTIONS

ECONOMY

MIGRATION

CONFLICT

CONTACT

TIME.
```

# 105. Cultural Persistence Off-Screen

Culture continues when players are absent.

Communities may:

- adapt practices
- develop slang
- create rituals
- abandon traditions
- reinterpret history
- absorb newcomers

The world does not freeze culturally off-screen.

# 107. Low-Resolution Culture

At low resolution, the system may preserve:

- dominant languages
- major rituals
- important norms
- symbolic identities
- significant cultural tensions
- broad local practices

It should not simulate every individual cultural action.

# 108. High-Resolution Culture

At high resolution, Culture may appear through:

- dialogue
- etiquette
- clothing
- food
- ritual
- humor
- expectations
- social consequence

Culture should be experienced through human behavior rather than exposition alone.

# 109. Cultural Persistence

Meaningful cultural patterns may persist across:

- sessions
- campaigns
- migration
- institutional change
- World States
- generations

But persistence must remain historically plausible.

---

# 110. Cultural Compression

The simulation does not need to preserve every cultural detail.

It should preserve what affects:

- identity
- expectation
- communication
- social interaction
- historical continuity
- conflict
- cooperation

This supports simulation economy.

Campaign consequences may become:

- warnings
- myths

# 114. Culture and Narrative

Narrative presents Culture.

Narrative does not own it.

Culture may be expressed through:

- speech
- behavior
- environment
- objects
- rituals
- expectations
- conflict
- humor
- history

Characters may know their own cultural context better than outsiders.

- origin
- minority practices
- subcultures
- older traditions

Cultural knowledge is not omniscient.

Aurora may possess large-scale cultural information.

- records
- observation
- communication
- available data

Living cultural meaning may change faster than records reflect.

Aurora may therefore hold outdated models.

Small changes accumulate.

# 121. Cultural Adaptation

Culture may adapt to new conditions.

But:

```text
CULTURAL ADAPTATION
≠
CULTURAL IMPROVEMENT.
```

A cultural practice may become useful under difficult conditions while creating other costs.

---

# 122. Cultural Resilience

Cultural resilience should not mean resistance to all change.

It may mean the ability to preserve meaningful continuity while adapting practices.

Conceptually:

```text

=

RESILIENCE.
```

# 123. Cultural Collapse

Avoid treating cultural collapse as automatic when institutions fail.

Culture may survive:

- governments
- economies
- communication networks
- formal institutions

through people, practice and memory.

---

# 124. Cultural Extinction

Some cultural practices may disappear entirely.

This should require plausible historical causality.

Avoid deleting Culture merely because simulation focus moves elsewhere.

# 125. Cultural Reemergence

Lost or dormant cultural practices may reappear through:

- surviving records
- family transmission
- migration
- archaeological discovery
- political revival
- renewed identity

Reemergence should not imply perfect restoration.

Future development must not turn Culture into:

- a Belief preset

- deterministic generational behavior
- automatic social conflict
- automatic social harmony

```text
WITHIN CULTURAL
CONTEXT X
```

Prefer:

```text

CREATED CONDITIONS
UNDER WHICH
EXISTING CULTURES
ADAPTED,
DIVERGED,
COMBINED
OR CHANGED.
```

# 137. Closing Principle

Culture is one of the ways humans carry meaning through time.

People inherit words.

Stories.

Songs.

Food.

Rituals.

Expectations.

Symbols.

Habits.

Ideas about what is normal.

Ideas about what is honorable.

Ideas about who belongs.

But inheritance is never the end of the process.

Humans reinterpret what they receive.

They preserve some things.

Reject others.

Combine old practices with new realities.

Pass changed versions forward.

History changes Culture.

Culture changes how humans experience history.

And individual humans remain capable of deciding what they will carry with them.

The central principle is:

> **Culture gives people shared ways of understanding the world, but it never removes the individual's ability to understand it differently.**
